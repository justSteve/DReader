The argumentIn brief9 paragraphsClick a claim to see his words

A German order-flow desk explains why it does not trade from a heatmap. The presenter grants the tool everything it actually does: it shows where large resting orders are and where they were, and it will tell you whether they were thick or thin. What it cannot show is how those orders were executed, and therefore who was doing the executing. For him that missing half is the whole game.

He opens by saying that institutional traders, and his own group at Trade the Traders, don't use heatmaps, and that the order books are the true market. But the argument that follows is not that the heatmap lies. It shows where the large orders are, and it can tell you whether they are "really big, very thick, and strong, or if they're just somewhat large." The objection is to what is left out.

Corrected 2026-09-06 · was “the sharpest conflict in the corpus”

But first, a number. To read size at all you need to know what large means in your instrument, and for the ES he says it: an order needs to be over 250 or 300 contracts, isolated and on its own, before you can judge it truly large; at 400, 500 or 600 you know. Carmine's series never puts a number on size. The nearest comparison is the podcast on this same channel, where 10,000 ES contracts bought in the book is the example of genuinely large activity: a different quantity, and an order of magnitude above.

Rests on 02:19Cited by 2 cards

Then the objection proper. When you look at a past block order on a heatmap, you have no idea how it was executed: large chunks or small, fast or slow. That is the information that tells you who was playing the market aggressively. It is exactly what Carmine says the heatmap's bubbles supply, so the dispute is narrower than its headline. Neither offers a statistic, so the corpus cannot adjudicate.

What the heatmap shows, and what large means02:19Watch here

“At the end of the day, the heat map shows you where the large orders are. Now, of course, you need to know how big they are. The heat map can show you if they're really big, very thick, and strong, or if they're just somewhat large. But honestly, if you don't really know the market and, for example, you don't know that in the S&P 500, that is in the ES future, an order needs to be over 250 or 300 contracts, isolated and on its own, for you to be able to judge that it's truly large. If you know it has 500 contracts, 400, 600, then you know it's really big. And yes, you can set all this information in a heat map and then you have it.”

Editor

The first size threshold anywhere in this corpus. Note the qualifier “isolated and on its own”: a single resting order, not a band and not a sum. For scale, the podcast on this channel uses 10,000 ES contracts bought as its example of genuinely large activity, and Carmine's Bookmap in episode three tags routine prints at 31 and 65. He gives the number for the ES only.

The objection03:01

“But what you don't have is when you look at past block orders that were placed somewhere, block orders from before, you have no idea how they were executed. Were they filled in large chunks, in small chunks, at high speed, at low speed? You can't immediately see that here at first. And that information is important for you because you need to know who was playing the markets aggressively there.”

Editor

The whole case in four sentences. Carmine's heatmap has bubbles for exactly this, executed aggression sized and coloured by side. But he credits speed to the DOM, never to the heatmap. The live disagreement is whether the bubble picture carries pace and intent. Neither man has measured it.
