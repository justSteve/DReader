# Read: The ONLY Market-Profile-Video you will EVER need

> **Market Profile as a map of time at price, and the two payoffs the corpus gets from nowhere else: the anomaly, where the most-traded price and the most-visited price diverge, and the rule that acceptance back inside an old value area tends to carry price across the whole profile.**

- **Channel:** Tom Vorwald EN / WorldClassEdge · **Uploaded:** 2025-11-30 · **Duration:** 15:06
- **Card:** [CARD.md](CARD.md) · **Watch:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1utSjHs_Mq8

## In brief

A 30-minute candlestick chart and a Market Profile are the same information
drawn two ways. That is where the presenter starts, and he spends the first
five minutes making sure you believe it: stack the 30-minute blocks at the
prices they touched and a mountain forms wherever the market lingered. The
peak is the point of control; the thin places are where price moved fast.

From there he builds a reading method out of time alone. Where the market
stayed, it felt comfortable; where it hurried, it did not, and hurried places
get revisited. The opening range tells you whether to expect a trend day or a
balanced one. A close outside the value area means something changed. Extremes
made of several letters at one price are weak and tend to be taken out; a tail
of single letters means strong traders ended the move.

Two things here are new to the corpus: the anomaly between the time-based and
volume-based points of control, and a near-rule about re-entering old value.

## The argument

The video's premise is that Market Profile is not a new idea and that nobody on
this channel invented it. He credits Peter Steidlmayer, a soybean trader in the
1980s, and frames the tool's purpose as the one Steidlmayer intended: to see
*where and who is determining the prices*. The angle he adds is a temporal one.
Where the market was fast, someone strong drove it. Where prices are slow, the
market is comfortable, and comfortable prices are very likely to be traded again
and even broken later.

He shows a 30-minute candlestick
chart, then the same candles lined up sideways at the prices they covered, then
expands and collapses the profile on screen so you can watch the two views turn
into each other. The market touched one price five or six times, another seven
times, and from those counts the points of control are derived: the price
ranges where the market spent the most time. This is the companion to the
channel's other profile lecture, [the Market Profile vocabulary video](../K8qtT2_axPo/CARD.md),
and the two agree on every definition they share.

The first genuinely new idea arrives when he overlays a volume profile on the
time profile. The value area from time and the value area from volume are
"sometimes a bit different", and on his example the market spent the most time
at one price while trading its highest volume at another. He calls this an
anomaly, and points out that a trader looking at Market Profile alone would
never see it. This is the operational payoff of a distinction the companion
video only names, [POC against VPOC](../K8qtT2_axPo/CARD.md): the two points of
control are worth separating precisely because the interesting places are where
they disagree. Nothing else in the corpus makes this move. Carmine Rosato and
the Smart Money Decode X series both use "POC" as one thing, and the channel's
own [volume profile lecture](../YmygDgtoxO8/CARD.md) treats the volume POC on
its own terms.

Then the reading rules, in a run. Single prints, one letter at a price, mean the
market never stayed there for even 30 minutes, and he is categorical that they
are "always initiated by strong market participants, because they have a reason
to create a push". Such ranges are often revisited to see whether that
participant still reacts. A close outside the value area says something changed
at the end of the day, and the next day's return to that balance is likely to be
rejected. That is the same close-based confirmation the PbD videos lean on,
where [a close outside value confirms the move out of balance](../jXR1afMy-3o/CARD.md)
and [closes rather than wicks establish acceptance](../Ly62G168MkQ/CARD.md).

His account of highs and lows is the corpus's constructive counterpart to the
channel's [rejection of stop hunting](../RwQBdF9TSvc/CARD.md). A buying tail is
at least three letters standing alone; a weak low is several prices established
inside one range. Weak extremes are very likely to be taken out again, not
because anyone hunts them but because "weak price areas are initiated by weak
traders". The level fails because of who built it. Carmine reaches a kindred
conclusion by a different route in his
[support and resistance episode](../5qBo04SMUFc/CARD.md): a bounce made by
aggressive buyers is a level waiting to break. He reads it from delta; this
channel reads it from the shape of the profile.

The fifth of his seven conclusions is the sentence the whole sweep was looking
for. If a market re-enters an old value area and finds acceptance there, "the
probability is high that the profile will be fully traversed". It has a trigger,
a condition and a target, which makes it the most rule-shaped statement on the
channel. It is also the theory behind the
[prior-day value area scalp](../zAwEX_tRUfE/CARD.md), which trades the same
geometry on a one-minute chart without saying why it should work. What is still
missing is what "acceptance" looks like in real time; for that he defers to
setups from the footprint and the order book, which is where
[the DOM and footprint videos](../usho6UVLqkE/CARD.md) and
[the order-flow guide](../vl01TiVTuoQ/CARD.md) pick up.

The sixth conclusion, that profiles divide into trend profiles and normal
profiles and that the division tells you whether the day carries a real trend,
is the seed of the framework that later becomes PbD, where
[P and b are the trend phases and D the balance phase](../m3IMdc7QwN4/CARD.md).

The video closes on route plans: from fair price to unfair price, from A to B,
when nothing in between would stop the major participants. That is context in
the sense Carmine gives the word in his
[context, location, confirmation rule](../7facFfjQ0UE/CARD.md), and, like
Carmine, he ends with the promise of "incredibly high risk-reward ratios"
without attaching a figure to any of it. No trade, stop, target or win rate
appears anywhere in the fifteen minutes.

## In his words

**What the profile is** *(01:42)*

"Before we dive deeper into market profile we first need to understand what it
actually shows us. And for that I've brought you this little picture here. These
are 30-minute candles. So a 30-minute candlestick chart, and each of these
candles gives us information about whether it was negative or positive. That's
all for now.

In market profile, here you see the corresponding time frame. We see exactly the
same picture, only that we've lined up the 30-minute blocks and placed them on
the appropriate axis, so that we can see over time how often the market has
spent those 30 minutes in a particular price range.

[...] In market profile we've simply summed up how much time, for example, the
market has spent in the price range of 6,714. And if you take a look at this in
the corresponding chart, you'll see that one, two, three, four, five times the
market touched this price range with 30-minute candles. And then you see in the
market profile one, two, three, four, five, six times the market touched this
price range there. That's why a kind of mountain forms here across the
accumulated time frames that the market profile provides us with. You can also
display each of these individual blocks as numbers or letters to see how far the
market moved within the corresponding 30-minute period."

> **Editor —** The chart is the S&P future. The card once recorded it as the
> Euro FX future on one run's guess; the two prices he reads aloud, 6,714 and
> 6,758, are index levels, and a frame of the chart header shows a CME
> contract in Sierra Chart dated 10-22-25. The card was corrected on 9-7-26.

**Points of control** *(04:14)*

"So the bottom line, and this is simply the most important thing, is that we
understand the market presents us with a profile here where the respective
30-minute candles are listed and summed up at the corresponding price ranges.
Here the market has touched 6,758 with 30-minute candles, for example, seven
times, and from that the so-called POCs are derived. And the POCs, these are the
so-called points of control, which are the price ranges where, according to the
market profile, the market spent the most time.

So how does that help us now? First of all, it helps us understand that here
supply and demand were time-based at a fair price level, probably here as well,
and here for example at this top. And in this price range, the market did not
stay for very long. And how does that help us? It clearly helps us, again from
the simplest logic: quick moves and longer stays immediately tell us that the
probability is high that the market feels comfortable here, and where it moves
quickly it does not feel comfortable."

**Initial balance** *(05:32)*

"Here you can see a typical chart from what's called market profiling. And each
of these blocks represents a day. So each of these profiles is made up of the
30-minute candles lined up over an entire day. [...] It had its trading opening
here. In the market profile, we can display what's called the initial balance,
showing how large the opening phase of trading was. This tells us whether we
should expect a more trending day at first or rather a balanced day. This means:
is it more likely that the market will establish itself steadily in a trend, or
will it swing back and forth? That's the first piece of information we get from
market profiling."

> **Editor —** The companion video pins the initial balance to the opening
> 30 minutes. Here he only says "the opening phase", and gives no rule for how
> wide is wide. The trend-or-balance call is asserted, not shown.

**The anomaly** *(06:22)*

"The next piece of information we get is: where are the value areas? And the
value areas from the market profile are sometimes a bit different from those of
the volume profile, because it can also happen, and you can see this very
clearly in this profile here since I have included the volume profile in this
chart, that the market had the highest trading volume up here but spent the
most time here. This means that in this window I can immediately see: aha, the
market did spend the most time here, but the highest trading volumes were traded
here. This is what's called an anomaly. If you were to look at all of this using
only the market profile, you wouldn't recognize this anomaly."

> **Editor —** This is the passage that earns the video its place in the
> corpus. The companion lecture separates the time-based point of control from
> the volume-based one and stops there; this is what the separation is for.
> He does not say what to do when the two diverge, only that you must be able
> to see it. Whether the divergence favours the volume price or the time price
> is left open.

**Single prints** *(07:07)*

"Here we see what's called a single print area. These are the blue lines here.
And single print areas, as the name suggests, are individual letters at a
certain price range. That means if you were to carefully look at the
corresponding 30-minute chart, you would clearly see that the price never
actually stayed at this specific level for more than 30 minutes. [...] And
single prints are always initiated by strong market participants, because they
have a reason to create a push. What does that mean for us in the future? And
you can see that beautifully here. Such price ranges are often revisited to see
if that strong market participant still reacts."

> **Editor —** "Always" is his word. It is the same move as the PbD videos'
> aggressive trend phase, where price leaves a level in one block and does not
> come back that day. The claim that the range gets revisited to test whether
> the participant still reacts is asserted from one example on screen.

**A close outside value** *(08:04)*

"We can immediately see, for example, when a market shifts into a certain price
range relationship. We can determine whether the closing prices were within the
balance, the value area, or if the closing price was outside. If it was outside,
then the information is clear for us: something changed at the close of trading.
And that's important, because if the market opens the next day and moves back
there, the probability is high that we will see a rejection at these balance
price areas."

**Tails and weak lows** *(08:54)*

"We know precisely how significant tops and how crucial lows are ultimately
formed. Were they formed with so-called buying tails, meaning at least three
distinct letters standing completely alone? Or were they formed with so-called
weak lows, indicating a different market dynamic, where the market has
established several prices within a single price range? This information in turn
gives us insight into whether the market will trade above or below these levels
again.

And always remember, especially with such weak lows or highs, you can see it
very clearly: the probability is very high that these will be taken out again,
because weak price areas are initiated by weak traders, and not, like those
buying tails or selling tails, by strong traders who have quickly created an
initiative move, which leads the market to simply give us information that this
could now be the end."

> **Editor —** Note the word: weak traders. He never says retail, and he never
> says who the weak traders are. The companion video calls the same formation a
> ledger and reads it as weak hands; this is the only place on the channel that
> gives the tail a size, at least three letters. Read alongside the podcast's
> denial that anyone is hunting stops, this is the constructive half of the
> argument: the low breaks because of who built it, not because of who is
> aiming at it.

**Seven conclusions** *(09:49)*

"So what is our conclusion when we analyze the market profile? First, we see
where the market is in balance or where the market initiates rapid movement.
Two, we can determine from the market open whether today is more likely to be a
trend day or a balance day. Three, we can identify through the selling and
buying tails where strong traders were active at the top or at the low. We can
recognize from the weak lows or highs whether these price areas are likely to be
revisited, or whether these price areas were rather initiated by strong market
participants, giving us the information that these levels will be crossed
again.

Fifth, and this is the most crucial point: we can use market profiling to create
context. We can clearly determine from where to where there is a high
probability that the big traders will drive their prices. Because if we know the
rules and understand that a market re-enters an old value area and finds
acceptance there, then we know there are rules that clearly tell us the
probability is high that the profile will be fully traversed.

And sixth, we will cover this in another important chapter. There are indeed
many different types of profiles, specifically what are known as trend profiles
and also what are called normal profiles, which can also be classified into
several distinct and important categories, which quite simply and clearly tell
us whether the overall market today should be thoroughly assessed as truly
having aggressive, strong and significant trends or not."

> **Editor —** The fifth point is the nearest thing to a tradeable rule in
> twenty-one videos: re-entry is the trigger, acceptance the condition, the far
> side of the profile the target. Two things are still absent. He does not
> define acceptance, and he does not say how often the traverse completes. The
> one-minute scalping video on this channel trades the prior day's value area
> on exactly this geometry and never states the rule; this is where it comes
> from. His "there are rules that clearly tell us" points at a body of Market
> Profile doctrine he does not reproduce here.

**Merging profiles** *(11:27)*

"And last but not least, seventh, we can also, and this is another great thing
about market profile, we can merge profiles. Meaning we can combine them and
create one large profile if we see that prices are consistently staying within
a fair range. [...] Here I have the value high and here I have the value low.
This means that fair and unfair prices can be found exactly within these price
ranges. If I want to use this the next day, I can refer to it first. But now
we've completed the next day and it traded exactly within these price ranges.
And this price range is also within that price range. So I add that as well.
And as you can see now, this changes the fair price range and you get new very
smart information through this merging. And this makes sense as long as a
market doesn't generate new shifts for itself."

> **Editor —** The merge is done through a German context menu on the
> platform, which Gemini read as *Profil mit vorherigem Profil verschmelzen*,
> merge the profile with the previous one. The stopping rule is the useful
> part: keep merging while the market stays inside the same fair range, stop
> when it shifts. That is a composite profile built by hand, one day at a time.

**Route plans** *(12:35)*

"To sum up, this means that we've been able to learn here who dominates the
lows, who was playing at the highs: weak, shallow highs. Onto the second point,
where did aggressive traders come into play? Which we can see here through the
single print areas. These give us great information for the future about where
the market likes to test again. Then, which balances do we have with the
so-called POCs, where the market has felt very comfortable with a high
probability? And we immediately know for each respective day where to narrow
down where 70% of the trades took place in the corresponding market profile,
since we have overlaid the market and volume profiles. [...] The market moved
from up here exactly to this price area and then corrected again. And of course
these are not coincidences but rather truly remarkable patterns.

Then we understand, for example, when we have such weak highs and strong lows,
that the probability is very high that something like this will be taken out
again. And with that, you can clearly and effectively construct comprehensive
route plans for yourself, explicitly stating that from areas of fair pricing to
levels of unfair pricing, the probability is significantly high that the market
will indeed move there, especially if the right and motivated sellers are
currently active and engaged in the trading process.

And we can predefine all of this for ourselves using the market profile, giving
us a predefined chart where we know exactly: this is the top, this is the
bottom, and the probability is high that the market will move from A to B,
because in between there is no information that would currently cause the
supply and demand levels of the major market participants to be an obstacle.
And that is precisely the big advantage. And if you now go ahead and start
combining these levels that you have cleverly constructed, for example by
integrating setups from the footprint chart or the order book, then with market
profiling you have found the perfect tool that gives you clear guidance on where
you can trade, and with which setups you can establish incredibly high
risk-reward ratios for yourself."

> **Editor —** The 70% is stated flat, as a share of the trades. The channel's
> volume-profile videos give the same container as 68.2%, one standard
> deviation, or 70%; the corpus's other sources never put a number on it at
> all. The closing promise of "incredibly high risk-reward ratios" is the only
> mention of risk or reward in the video and carries no figure. The method is
> a map of where; the footprint and the order book are left to supply when.
