## In brief

Twelve minutes on what an order book is and how to read one. It opens with the
best one-sentence definition of order flow in this corpus: a chart tells you
where prices were; order flow tells you who's buying, who's selling, and how
aggressively. It climbs from candlestick to volume to footprint to the live
book and rests on one premise: liquidity is what the market likes to seek, so
a large resting order is a destination.

What makes it worth reading is the calibration. He puts the books side by
side: a 325-contract ask on the S&P, single-digit rows on the DAX, five and
six thousand a side in the bond futures, twos and fours and sixes in gold.
"Large" is a per-market quantity, and nothing else in the corpus shows it
across this many markets at once.

There is no trade and no risk number. The entry promised in the opening is a
fill.

## The argument

Three promises open the video: what order flow is, why professionals will not
trade without it, and a live entry you would never see on a standard chart. It
delivers the first fully, the second by demonstration and the third not at
all.

The definition is the reason the card exists. A chart shows where prices were;
order flow shows who is buying, who is selling, and how aggressively. Carmine
Rosato's second episode builds the same premise from a Time & Sales slide:
every transaction has a buyer and a seller, and the tape tells you which one
was the aggressor. This presenter gets there in one sentence, and neither man
cites the other. A second line is nearly as good: you no longer see the market
as a line, but as a decision.

Then the ladder of instruments. The candlestick gives price; volume under it
gives how much traded and when; a footprint splits that volume into buys and
sells at each price, the first view of where liquidity is thick and thin. But
the footprint is history. "Pure order flow", in his phrase, is the book: limit
orders resting right now on the bid, offered to buy, and on the ask, offered
to sell, and the first thing it tells you is which side is heavier. The
premise that makes this a method is dropped in as an aside, never argued:
liquidity is always what the market likes to seek.

What he means by liquidity matters, because two other schools in the corpus
use the word for something else. For Smart Money Decode X and Trading Notes, a
liquidity pool is the stops presumed to sit beyond a swing high or low, and
the method is to wait for the sweep. This channel's own Wall Street scalper
video calls that a retail myth and points at single-digit prints below the
lows. Here, liquidity is resting size you can see in the book. Same word,
different object, and this video only ever means the visible one.

The method is calibration. Before a large order means anything you must know
what large is here, so he shows the books: a 325-contract ask on the S&P; a
DAX ladder of 1, 1, 2, 4, 3, 5, 33; crude oil about as thin as the DAX; bond
futures with five and six thousand a side; gold offering twos, fours and
sixes. The channel's heatmap video puts the ES threshold at over 250 to 300
contracts, and its companion order-flow video shows a 676-contract ES bid and
a DAX ladder of 2, 4, 8, 11, 7. Together they are the corpus's reference for a
large resting order, market by market. Carmine does the same for a different
quantity: in his third episode he disclaims a fixed delta number, then reads
the session's typical range, 200 to 500, and calls anything over a thousand an
outlier. Same discipline, different variable: Carmine calibrates aggression,
this presenter resting size.

The one composite setup pairs a large order with the volume profile. Areas of
little turnover are where rejection happens and where movement is fast,
because supply and demand were never filled there and the big participants
have no interest in holding the market in balance. A large order above a thin
area therefore gives a destination and a speed: price will probably run
through the thin zone to the order, quickly. Carmine keeps a footprint column
to see the same lack of interest; the channel's Market Profile videos call it
single prints. The companion video names the move front-running, the resting
order as a magnet, and it is the one thing in this channel's method Carmine
never does: he fades absorption at a level and does not trade toward size.

Spoofing is handled without dodging. It existed and probably still does; an
order can be pulled. But exponentially large orders are usually real, because
someone has the ambition to do something there. He declines to argue why and
says so. The heatmap video is firmer, calling spoofing illegal, and the
argument he defers has not turned up in any of the twenty-one videos carded
from this channel.

After the fill comes the closest thing to confirmation. Mark the level. Ask
whether the block was an entry or an exit, and whether it filled at once or
piece by piece, because that says who is active. As long as price holds above
the level with buying still fast, a larger participant probably has more to
do, and such levels often become balance areas as the market keeps checking
that the liquidity is still there. That is Carmine's retest logic on a filled
order. But the questions are posed, not answered, and the closing checklist,
locate big orders, read speed per product, mark high-volume levels and re-
check them, is the step-by-step guide of the title. It carries no threshold,
no stop and no target.
