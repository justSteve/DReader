## In brief

VWAP, explained from the definition up and then argued against its most
popular use. The presenter's VWAP is a session-anchored, volume-weighted
average that resets every trading day. Price returns to it often, and a whole
family of strategies and algorithms is built on the assumption that it always
will. His point is that "often" is a statement about regime, not about VWAP.

In balance, large participants want to trade near the average, so the return is
probable. Once a market trends, new fair value forms away from the old
equilibrium and the return may not come at all. Adding to a counter-trend
position while waiting for it is how a high hit rate turns into one ruinous
loss. So: classify structure first, read market profile for single prints,
take the bounce only in balance, treat VWAP as a target rather than a reason,
and in a trend look for continuation off it instead.

It is the companion to the channel's other VWAP video, made seven months
earlier, and it reaches the same rule from the risk side.

## The argument

He starts with what the line is. VWAP is "nothing more than a volume weighted
moving average", built from time and volume, and it shows where the average
trade in the period you are looking at was done. The calculation is session
based: a new day begins and VWAP resets to zero, which is why the line jumps at
the open. A continuous, multi-day VWAP can be displayed, but he does not think
it useful, and he prefers to read the line during a product's main hours, where
the volume is heaviest. He then puts it beside the volume profile, VPOC and
value area, because the two describe the same thing from different angles: one
is the average of the session's trade, the other is where the session's trade
piled up.

The behaviour he wants you to notice comes next. In a sideways phase VWAP
behaves like any moving average and price oscillates around it. When the
market starts trending, price leaves and the first-order probability is that
it does not come straight back. His explanation of why the return happens at
all is the most useful sentence in the video: large participants prefer a
market in equilibrium because "that's where they can calmly build up and
unwind their positions". That is the auction premise Carmine Rosato builds his
whole series on in [the foundation episode](../QaNPAaEnB5E/CARD.md), stated
here as a reason to watch VWAP in balance phases and, by implication, to stop
trusting it once balance breaks.

The danger is then drawn out on the canvas. Algorithms, he says, buy a fixed
distance below VWAP expecting a return to equilibrium. When the market instead
builds volume below the value area, fair value has moved, the large
participants who were positioned at the old equilibrium "now have a problem",
and continuation is the likely outcome. The trader who bought the first dip,
then the second, now has no coherent way to manage risk. He ties it to a
principle he has used before: the market does not always return to the point
of control either, and it does not always return to VWAP for the same reason.

How do you know which regime you are in? He offers three checks. Market
profile single prints, which say aggressive participants have entered and are
dominating, so "caution is advised" with any counter strategy. Prior support
and resistance from earlier periods. And, when you do take a counter-trend
setup, VWAP is a sensible profit target rather than a thesis, because the large
players read it as a bias line: below it is short, above it is long. The
profile reading leans on the market profile material the channel teaches in
[its Market Profile explainer](../1utSjHs_Mq8/CARD.md), which is where single
prints are introduced.

The numbers arrive late and are modest. The market returns to the point of
control to regain balance "about 70%, a little over 70% of the time". A true
trend day, one that closes at its extreme, is "about 20% of trading days for
many products". The channel elsewhere gives the trend share as roughly 30%, and
the two figures are not reconciled, but they are also not quite the same
measure: a day that closes on its low is a subset of days that fail to balance.

The close is the reason to read this video. Take the bounce at VWAP when the
structure is sideways and in balance. Judge the trend from the opening phase,
the profile shapes and the day's news. Do not pyramid. "You make 20 trades that
work out and then on the 21st trade some news comes out, it wipes out your
entire position." That is negative skew stated plainly, a high hit rate
concealing a fat left tail, and it is the counter-argument to the channel's own
[80 to 90% win-rate video](../uFxYcpiaOpw/CARD.md), which describes its setup
in full and its risk not at all. It is also why the statistics in
[the volume-profile video](../NkQeOVDTAec/CARD.md) insist on a risk-reward
figure beside the hit rate.

His final point is the mirror image of the mean-reversion rule: when a market is
strongly trending, stop trying to get back to VWAP and look instead for a trade
at VWAP that continues the trend. That is the pull-back rule the channel states
as Rule 1 in [the later VWAP video](../43JaKHRvxHk/CARD.md), and it is Carmine's
[buy the pullback, never the break](../w7tvJCuZAq8/CARD.md) in a different
vocabulary. Between them, the two VWAP videos supply the mode-switching rule the
[1-minute scalping card](../zAwEX_tRUfE/CARD.md) found missing: the same line is
a target in balance and an entry in trend, and the choice is made by classifying
structure before the trade, not by the indicator.
