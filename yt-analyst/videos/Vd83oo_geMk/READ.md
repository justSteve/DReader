# Read: THE TRUTH about Heatmap & Liquidity

> **The case against heatmaps from an order-book desk, and the corpus's first concrete number for what counts as a large order: over 250 to 300 ES contracts.**

- **Channel:** Tom Vorwald EN / WorldClassEdge · **Uploaded:** 2025-11-07 · **Duration:** 15:13
- **Card:** [CARD.md](CARD.md) · **Watch:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vd83oo_geMk

## In brief

A German order-flow desk explains why it does not trade from a heatmap. The
presenter grants the tool everything it actually does: it shows where large
resting orders are and where they were, and it will tell you whether they
were thick or thin. What it cannot show is how those orders were executed, in
what chunks and at what speed, and therefore who was doing the executing. For
him that missing half is the whole game. His answer is the order book, read
live, with a footprint and a market profile beside it telling him which kind
of participant is active right now.

This matters to us because Carmine Rosato's series makes the Bookmap heatmap a
core instrument. Two practitioners, one object, opposite verdicts, and no
statistics on either side. The video also gives the corpus its first concrete
size threshold for the ES book, and a second independent definition of an
iceberg order.

## The argument

He opens by saying that institutional traders, and his own group at Trade the
Traders, don't use heatmaps, and that the order books are the true market. But
the argument that follows is not that the heatmap lies. It shows where the
large orders are, and it can tell you whether they are "really big, very
thick, and strong, or if they're just somewhat large." He even concedes a heatmap
can be set to carry the same size information his book does. The
objection is to what is left out.

But first, a number. To read size at all you need to know what large
means in your instrument, and for the ES he says it: an order needs to be over
250 or 300 contracts, isolated and on its own, before you can judge it truly
large; at 400, 500 or 600 you know. Carmine's series never puts a number on
size. The nearest comparison is the podcast on this same channel,
where 10,000 ES contracts bought in the book is the example of genuinely large
activity: a different quantity, a stretch of executed buying rather than one
resting order, consistent, and an order of magnitude above.

Then the objection proper. When you look at a past block order on a heatmap,
you have no idea how it was executed: large chunks or small, fast or slow. That
is the information that tells you who was playing the market aggressively.
It is exactly what Carmine says the
heatmap's bubbles supply, executed aggression drawn against resting bands, so
the dispute is narrower than its headline. Carmine credits speed to the DOM
rather than the heatmap, and his episode-three example of a resting bid that
pulled before price reached it is this presenter's objection spoken from the
other side. What remains contested is whether the bubble picture carries pace
and intent, or whether you have to watch the individual trades print. Neither
offers a statistic, so the corpus cannot adjudicate.

What he reads instead comes in three parts: whether more is trading into the
bid or the ask, what speed underlies it, and how the market reacts when it
meets a large order. It is true that a market seeks
liquidity, he says, but what environment are we in? His market profile
answers that: the market touched yesterday's highs, is now repairing its
single prints, and has dropped below the value area without accelerating, so
only small players are active. This is the channel's Market Profile vocabulary
used not to find a level but to identify who is in the market.

Delta is on his screen: the footprint carries a delta histogram and he reads
it, but never attaches a number. On this one
point Carmine is the more concrete: in episode three he disclaims a fixed
threshold and then demonstrates a session baseline, a few hundred as the day's
normal range and anything over a thousand an outlier. On delta Carmine
supplies the method; on size this channel supplies the number. Each fills the
other's gap.

The second half is about the trader who reads the heatmap and trades toward
it. Someone sees a lot of liquidity above, goes long because those orders
"absolutely have to be eaten up," is stopped out twice, and then the market
takes the liquidity without them. Or he scales in, positions one, two and
three, down a staircase of ranges before the crash. The first is this channel's
own front-running idea, the resting order as a magnet, shown failing on
timing. Notice the concession: the market
"actually grabs the big liquidity." The sweep that the podcast on this channel
calls a retail myth is here something that happens; the heatmap cannot
time it, because it cannot say whether the players who would take it are
present.

So the verdict is conditional. At the open the probabilities are higher than
mid-session; in stable phases the market sits in balance and often does not
pick up those large orders. He grants that the heatmap can predefine
levels the market might move to. Heatmaps are fine, he closes, if you are
scanning many markets or cannot find the big liquidity, but not a good way to
build an edge.

Two definitions round it out. Spoofing, placing orders and pulling them before
price arrives, is "absolutely forbidden," and in the same breath it does
happen sometimes. That is the position of his step-by-step guide too, where
spoofing "probably still does" exist; the two videos differ in emphasis, not
substance. And the iceberg: a participant constantly routing large orders into
a position while not much changes. Trading Notes defines the same object as an
order that reloads at one level, and Carmine's second episode reads it as
absorption without naming it. Two sources name it, a third describes the
mechanism unnamed — no shared citation, one concept.

**Trading toward the wall** *(09:30)*

> "What happens if you're someone who constantly tries to trade in the
> direction of the large orders? You just get totally crushed if you think that
> these orders absolutely have to be eaten up. I know traders who do the
> following. For example, they go and say up here there's a lot of liquidity.
> Now we've had the opening phase here. After the market opening, the market
> immediately began to surge upward, taking off rapidly. Now they start at the
> latest here. They go long here. They get stopped out. They go long again.
> Here they get stopped out again. And eventually they've lost their money.
> And then the market does what it likes to do when there's a lack of
> liquidity and it moves up here and actually grabs the big liquidity."

> **Editor —** This is the front-running idea from his own orderflow-system
> video, the large resting order as a magnet, shown failing. The magnet is
> real; the arrival is not scheduled. And read the last sentence against the
> corpus: the market goes and takes the resting liquidity. Trading Notes builds
> a whole method on waiting for that grab. This presenter agrees it happens
> and says the heatmap cannot tell you when, because it cannot tell you who is
> present to do it.

**Scaling into the ranges** *(10:33)*

> "But there are also phases where the market just ranges. Crashes down, forms
> the next range, crashes down again, forms the next range, and so on. And
> then traders who trade like this tend to do the following, and simply build
> the first position here, the second position here, the third position here,
> and eventually there's a big crash and the account takes a heavy drawdown.
> And that's the big danger if you can't read which market participants are
> actually active in the market at the moment."

**Context and regime** *(11:05)*

> "The heat map is a tool that can help me identify where big orders are and
> where big orders have been. We understand that liquidity is always
> determined by the market. But who is currently moving toward liquidity? Big
> players or small ones? Is that liquidity actually being taken or not? That
> only works if you have context. For example, right at the market open, you
> have higher probabilities than in the middle of the session when only the
> small market participants are currently active, or how extended are the
> respective opening phases. This gives you the opportunity to predefine
> certain levels using the heat map where the market might move to or not. And
> all of this information needs to be connected with each other."

> "As long as the market is in stable phases, it will always be in balance and
> often won't pick up those large orders. It only does that at certain times or
> especially in certain market phases. And then I can look here in the order
> books, see very clearly who's dominant, what's being played here, which
> market participants are currently being aggressive, or which market
> participants aren't present at all."

> **Editor —** The balance and imbalance frame the rest of the channel teaches
> as PbD, applied to resting size: in balance the walls mostly stand, and it
> takes an imbalance phase, or the open, for them to be taken. Note the
> concession in the middle of the case against: the heatmap can predefine
> levels. His verdict is about what it cannot do, not that it does nothing.

**The conclusion** *(12:48)*

> "Yes, it can help you identify in the markets where large orders are, where
> large orders were and what happened afterwards, if you look back at the
> past. But can you really see who was actually dominant at the moment such a
> large order was placed? And can you see whether there's a high probability
> that these market participants have already offloaded that order block?
> That's going to be difficult."

**Individual trades, and the iceberg** *(13:33)*

> "My dear Serge always said, you have to learn to paint the chart in your
> mind. Then you'll also be able to understand what's happening in the
> markets. And then you'll recognize which key figures are being played in
> which market. For example, by larger market participants, because here you
> see individual trades, not aggregated orders, but actual individual trades.
> And you can observe them. If you notice that a market participant is
> constantly routing large orders into a position, but you see that not much is
> changing, you know, yes, that's an iceberg order. They just keep stacking up
> more orders there. If you see that a lot is being absorbed or something
> similar, in my opinion, that simply gives you much, much more and better
> information than if you only have a heat map that just provides you with
> these small fragments."

> **Editor —** The second independent arrival on the iceberg in this corpus
> (with Trading Notes). Carmine describes the same mechanism as absorption and
> never uses the word — an earlier draft counted him as a third.
> Trading Notes defines it as an order that shows a small fraction and reloads
> at the same level; Carmine's second episode reads the same thing as
> absorption, heavy aggression with no follow-through, without using the word.
> The mentor's name is caption-rendered and unverified.

**Last word** *(14:49)*

> "Heat maps are cool if you're starting to look at a lot of markets or if you
> can't quite figure out where the big liquidity is. But in my opinion, it's
> not a good way to build an edge. For that, you simply need to understand
> more context."
