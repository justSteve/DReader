## In brief

Not a tour of the volume profile but a way of thinking with it. A high-volume
node, the presenter says, tells you nothing on its own except that something
could happen there. That possibility is an idea, and an idea is not a trade. It
needs proof: a setup at the level showing the players are reacting now. A setup, in his definition, is any formation of price, volume or
footprint whose only job is to keep the risk small. Entry is the pullback after the
market leaves it, the target is the next high-volume node, and the distance
between them is the risk-reward.

Then he asks the question the rest of this corpus avoids: do you know your key
figures? At least 200 to 300 trades before you have any; then, in his worked
example, a hit rate of 50% and an average risk-reward of "maybe a two". Nine
and a half minutes, and the corpus's only pairing of a hit rate with a
risk-reward.

## The argument

He opens with a definition small enough to hold in one hand. A footprint is
nothing but market movement, price movement and the volume the players traded
to make it. Far more than 95% of that volume, he says, is placed by major
participants, and retail's job is to plan around them rather than derive
"hocus-pocus setups" from them. The figure is unsourced, but the premise beneath it is the one Carmine Rosato's
[Trading Orderflow Series](../../playlists/orderflow-series-PLWQioWs8oOiFKQnwIIYbw8N7ks7d-UAPQ.md)
rests on: the flow worth reading is institutional, and retail is the
liquidity.

The profile itself gets an equally spare treatment: summed volume at each
price. Asked how that helps, he answers "at first, not at all." It becomes
useful only once you select, high volume here, low volume there, and notice
that the market tends to react on returning to where a lot of liquidity
traded. A level lets you expect a reaction. "That's all we can do. We can only ever expect that
something will happen there." The expectation is an idea, and an idea is not a
trade.

The trade needs proof: a reaction from the players actually occurring at the
level, now. Always both. Set beside Carmine's Context, Location,
Confirmation rule from [episode 7](../7facFfjQ0UE/CARD.md), this is the last two
letters: the node is Location, the proof is Confirmation.
Context is absent: nothing here classifies the market before the level is
drawn. The channel has that step in its [VWAP video](../43JaKHRvxHk/CARD.md),
where structure is read first and decides whether a level is a pull-back entry
or a mean-reversion target, but a viewer of this video alone never sees it.

His scenario is a move up into a high-volume area. There "a reaction is
usually a counter at first," a retracement, which he plans from that level to
the next major volume. Both halves agree with Carmine
without citing him: reversal-first on the first test of a level, from
[episode 6](../w7tvJCuZAq8/CARD.md), and the target at the opposing level. One
setup for all occasions he dismisses as "nonsense".

Then the definition the video should be remembered for. A setup is "a formation
of price or volume or a footprint or whatever that gives me an advantage in
keeping my risk smaller." It is defined by what it does to the stop, not by what
it predicts, and the instrument does not matter. His worked example is an
inside bar at the level, the market lingering and deciding, backed by volume;
a high and a low; and when the market moves away and pulls back, that pullback
is the entry. Here he and
Carmine agree and part in the same breath. The pullback entry is Carmine's
break-and-retest, and the opposite of this channel's own
[1-minute scalping video](../zAwEX_tRUfE/CARD.md), which enters on the initial
break; no video on the channel says which to use. But Carmine reads
confirmation on the live tape, never on a candle close, and this example is a
candle pattern. The proof slot is left generic; the volume exhaustion of the
channel's [multi-timeframe video](../HySZZSjMxF8/CARD.md) and Carmine's
absorption and volume tail from [episode 2](../eJZhX6Xz4cU/CARD.md) are the
concrete instruments that would fill it.

With a stop below the setup and a target at the next node, the
risk-to-profit distance falls out, and he asks what the rest of this corpus
never does: do you know your key figures? The one flat statement: at least
200 to 300 trades before you have any. The rest is a worked example, hedged: if
you should reach a hit rate of 50%, where a hit means the
market reached the target from the entry, then you know your average
risk-reward, "maybe a two". At 50% the break-even ratio is one, so a two would
yield half an R per trade in expectancy. Carmine's eight episodes never state a
win rate, so even hedged this is the corpus's only pairing of a hit rate
with a ratio. This channel's own
[80–90% win-rate video](../uFxYcpiaOpw/CARD.md) states one but never its ratio;
[AlgoTrade Pro](../UL5QOCSKnU0/CARD.md) reports a Sharpe of 4.19 beside a
−75.42% drawdown. This video's test, a sample large enough and the
ratio beside the rate, is the one both fail.

He closes on a weekly profile and admits, over his own chart, "Did it give me a
setup here? I don't see that in hindsight at all." The level gave an idea; the
chart gave no proof; no trade. The method is the same at any scale, node to node,
the geometry behind the [market-profile video's](../1utSjHs_Mq8/CARD.md) rule
that an accepted re-entry into a value area tends to traverse the whole
profile. What is missing is missing everywhere in the corpus: no
position size, no volume threshold to turn "backed by volumes" into a test, no
regime. Without your own tracked setups, he says, "trading is always like
flying blind."
