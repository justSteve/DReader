# Read: The Simplest Day Trading Strategy for Beginners (PbD Method)

> **PbD taught from zero: find the impulse, then the sideways phase that follows it, read both from candle closes rather than wicks, and understand why large money prefers the sideways phase in the first place.**

- **Channel:** Tom Vorwald EN / WorldClassEdge · **Uploaded:** 2026-07-31 · **Duration:** 16:10
- **Card:** [CARD.md](CARD.md) · **Watch:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ly62G168MkQ

## In brief

The channel's PbD method with the profile vocabulary stripped out, drawn by
hand for someone who has never read a chart. It rests on one distinction: a
market is either trending, which he calls imbalance, or sideways, which he
calls equilibrium. The reason that distinction matters is an order-flow reason. In a sideways phase a great deal of
liquidity gathers at each price, so a large participant can build a position
calmly at a fair price. In a fast phase very little trades at any single level,
so the same participant is forced to pay up. That is why big money likes
sideways phases, and why the sideways phase that forms after an impulse is
where he looks for the trend to continue.

The rest is procedure: locate the momentum, count the closes, mark the range
that follows, and treat a breakout from it, or a return to it, as the trade.
His one number: sideways phases are about 70% of the market.

## The argument

He starts with a line chart and a question: what happened here? PbD, he says,
lets you narrow down where the market had momentum and where it was in
equilibrium. That is the entire classification. Carmine Rosato opens his series
on the same premise, balance and imbalance as the two states of an auction
([QaNPAaEnB5E](../QaNPAaEnB5E/CARD.md)); neither cites the other. This video
adds a beginner's explanation of why the split is not cosmetic.

The explanation is about liquidity. In a sideways phase a relatively large
amount of liquidity gathers, and markets can "conduct their business in
peace". In a fast phase very little can be traded at any one price, so a seller
who wanted a predefined level ends up selling at prices that look too cheap to
him, or a buyer chasing a rally has to buy high. Large traders therefore prefer
the sideways phase, and he ties this to the calendar: inflows at the start of a
month or quarter want a solid market phase to be placed in. It is the closest
the beginner video comes to the order book.

Then the rule that sets this channel apart: PbD does not use absolute highs
and lows. It uses closing prices and openings, because
on larger timeframes most of the turnover took place inside the candle body,
and the turnover in the spikes was quickly rejected. This is the second
independent statement of the close rule; the fullest one, with closes locating
the break-off edges of a profile, is in
[jXR1afMy-3o](../jXR1afMy-3o/CARD.md). It also puts him squarely against
Carmine, whose confirmation lives on the live tape and who argues that waiting
for a close turns a small stop into a large one
([7facFfjQ0UE](../7facFfjQ0UE/CARD.md)). The two schools agree on what
acceptance means and disagree on how soon you are allowed to believe it.

The procedure follows. First an impulse, which he defines as a move with no
large pullbacks, straight in one direction. Then, and he calls this the most
crucial step, the sideways phase that forms after it. An upward impulse that
settles into a sideways phase is a P: a market imbalance on the buy side, more
buyers than sellers, with the liquidity now sitting up at the new prices. The
letters are defined in his own words in
[m3IMdc7QwN4](../m3IMdc7QwN4/CARD.md): P and b are aggressive trend phases, D
a balance phase. The gloss that the letters are the shapes a profile draws is
ours, not his; he gestures at it here, sketching what a P's volume profile
"would look something like", but never says shape.

From a P two trades follow. If the market breaks out upward again from the
sideways phase, ideally on a closing price, the initial assumption is trend
continuation, because the market has already accepted higher prices with
liquidity. And if the market comes back down to that old liquidity, he
initially assumes it will continue to run from there, because it told him up
top that it was accepting the breakout. The second half is Carmine's
break-and-retest rule from the other direction: buy the defended retest
([w7tvJCuZAq8](../w7tvJCuZAq8/CARD.md)). The first half is where they part;
Carmine never buys the initial break and fades its first test. The one-minute version of this same setup, with the 80 to 90% hit
rate attached and the risk left out, is
[uFxYcpiaOpw](../uFxYcpiaOpw/CARD.md).

The chart walk-through supplies the counting method, the closest the channel
comes to a mechanical rule. Momentum gives higher closes; the count ends when
lower closes arrive; the range that follows is the sideways phase. The same in
reverse for a b. A later breakout, a test, a rejection at the test: these are
the trades, all located by where the acceleration was.

The D turns the trend method into a range method. After a dynamic move, the
market plays up and down until a large sideways phase emerges, valid until a
previous price range is traded away. A market that has left a long D
"really likes to run back into this fair price range", which is the return to
value the channel puts at about 70% of phases in
[YmygDgtoxO8](../YmygDgtoxO8/CARD.md). And inside a D you do not follow the
trend, you counter-trade it, short at the top and long at the bottom, until an
impulse removes the range. The D is also his answer to failure: stopped out of
a P or b, expect a larger balance game and switch to the counter-trader's
tools.

The closing figure, sideways phases at about 70% of the market, is the round
number of [K8qtT2_axPo](../K8qtT2_axPo/CARD.md) and the rest of the channel,
as unsourced here as everywhere else. The high hit rates, he adds, live in the
fast P and b phases; he does not say what they are. No live trade, no stop, no
target, no risk-reward.

> the first thing we need to understand. We distinguish between active trend
> structures where markets are in imbalance from a supply and demand
> perspective and sideways phases where the market is in equilibrium. In the
> second step, we naturally need to know how do I even come to say that this is
> meaningful in any way?"

**Why large money prefers the sideways phase** *(02:29)*

> "The logic behind it is that in these sideways phases a relatively large
> amount of liquidity is usually gathered and in such a fast market phase
> relatively little liquidity can usually be traded at a single price level.
> What does that mean for us? For us it simply means that we can make an
> assumption that for the most part in these sideways phases markets are able
> to conduct their business in peace. That means entering positions calmly and
> at relatively constant fair prices.
>
> In the second step, this means that in these fast market phases it's very
> difficult, for example for a seller to be able to sell at a predefined price
> level at all. And then in the end this trader has to sell at prices that
> might seem too expensive to him because he might have wanted to make his
> sales at higher prices instead. [...] A seller or, if it goes up quickly, a
> buyer doesn't have the opportunity to place something on the market at a
> predefined price range.
>
> And that's why large traders naturally really like these sideways phases,
> because if they want to place a lot of liquidity in the market, like right
> now, for example, on the first or second of the quarterly opening season,
> there are inflows of funds, they naturally have more of an interest in doing
> the whole thing in a solid market phase where they can get their stocks or
> whatever product they want to buy at a fair price in peace. If the market
> now [...] moves very quickly in one direction, upwards, then they have to
> buy at a high price. So, that's the first thing I'm basing the whole concept
> on in terms of thoughts."

> **Editor —** This is the best plain-language statement on the channel of
> why balance is where positions get built. The order-book videos
> ([usho6UVLqkE](../usho6UVLqkE/CARD.md),
> [vl01TiVTuoQ](../vl01TiVTuoQ/CARD.md)) show the same thing as resting size;
> here it is told as a seller's problem, which is how a beginner will
> actually understand it.
