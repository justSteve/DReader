# Read: The Three Participants Nobody Tells You About

> **The cast list behind the smart-money method: retail is the fuel, institutions are too large to fill without engineering the conditions, and a stop hunt is a market maker rebalancing its book, not a grudge.**

- **Channel:** Smart Money Decode X · **Uploaded:** 2026-08-07 · **Duration:** 11:47
- **Card:** [CARD.md](CARD.md) · **Watch:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7F9wBYse-Nk

## In brief

Video three of the series names who is on the other side of a trade and gives
each of them a motive. Retail traders matter for the liquidity they provide,
not the capital they bring: everyone learned the same patterns from the same
resources, so their stops, entries and targets sit in predictable places.
Institutions have one problem, size. A large order moves price against itself,
so they wait for panic, absorb the selling for weeks, then push price below
obvious support to collect the last stops before the markup begins. Market
makers are a business that quotes both sides and lives on the spread; when
retail piles onto one side of their book, moving price to where the opposite
flow will appear is what a stop hunt mechanically is.

No chart is read and no number you could trade is given. What the video
contributes is the motive layer the rest of the series builds tools for, and
the corpus's clearest "yes, but mechanically" answer to the stop-hunting
question.

## The argument

The premise is stated as a provocation and then defended as mechanics: someone
with more capital, more information and more patience is on the other side of
your trade, and they positioned before you did because your behaviour is
predictable. He is careful to call this "not a conspiracy theory" but a
description of how markets function, and the whole video is an attempt to earn
that framing by giving each participant a motive that does not require malice.

Retail comes first, and the distinction he wants is that retail is not the
target but the fuel. The reasoning is that a trader working from public
information and widely taught patterns will place stops just under round
numbers, buy the break of a prior high and go long a hammer at support, and
because everyone does, those orders are located before they are placed. A stop
becomes a market order the instant it triggers; a breakout entry is exit
liquidity for whoever positioned before the break. This is Carmine Rosato's
premise episode almost sentence for sentence, [retail as the bait
fish](../0QlGCz6U_1g/CARD.md), and the two schools agree on it completely. The
Vorwald channel's volume-profile video reaches the same conclusion from the
supply side, arguing that [most readable volume is large participants
discharging an obligation to route orders](../NkQeOVDTAec/CARD.md), which is
the institution's size problem seen from the tape.

That size problem is the centre of the video. A large buyer moves price against
itself with every fill, so it cannot simply buy when it wants to; it must
engineer conditions in which supply is abundant. The sequence he then narrates,
waiting for a prolonged decline into obvious support, absorbing panic selling
for days or weeks while the chart looks boring, then pushing price through the
support to trigger the last cluster of stops and absorb the shorts who chase
the breakdown, is Wyckoff's accumulation, and he says so. It is also exactly
what Carmine's footprint episode shows on the tape as [aggression that fails
to move price](../eJZhX6Xz4cU/CARD.md), and what his volume episode trades as
[the pullback that absorbs the breakout volume](../w7tvJCuZAq8/CARD.md). The
difference is that this video tells the story and gives you no way to see it
happening; the footprint is where you would look.

The market-maker section is the most useful and the one that needs the most
translation for an S&P trader. His market maker is a business with no
directional opinion, quoting a bid and an ask, earning the spread, and trying
to keep a balanced book. The nuance he adds is that when retail is
one-directional the book is unbalanced, and the cheapest way to find the
opposite flow is to move price to where it will naturally appear. That, he
says, looks exactly like stop hunting, and mechanically it is; the motive is
inventory, not hatred. This is the corpus's most explicit answer to a question
three other sources argue over. The [Vorwald podcast](../RwQBdF9TSvc/CARD.md)
concedes the mechanical stop-out but denies that anyone engineers it, and
brings the one checkable fact: the trades printing under lows are often
single-digit, so the size a hunter would need is not there. [Trading
Notes](../IUWvHVout94/CARD.md) opens on "nobody is hunting your stops" and then
trades the sweep anyway. This video's market-maker version is closer to the
podcast than its title suggests, a neutral business rebalancing; it is the
institutional version, an engineered final liquidity grab, that the podcast's
evidence actually disputes.

Two things in his model do not transfer to the ES. First, "three roles, one
entity: your broker, your counterparty, a neutral participant" describes a
dealing-desk broker in spot forex or CFDs. On a centrally cleared futures
exchange your broker is not your counterparty and the clearing house stands
between every trade; the market makers are separate liquidity providers in the
same order book as you. Second, stops are not literally visible in a central
limit order book. The claim that "your stops are visible to them" reduces to
the weaker one that everyone knows where retail puts them, which is the half of
the thesis the podcast's guest also concedes. The predictability argument
survives that translation; the surveillance imagery does not.

What the video does not do is tell you how to recognise any of this: what
counts as a prolonged decline, how to locate obvious support, how an unbalanced
book shows itself. It promises the rest of the series as the toolkit, and the
corpus has the pieces: [video 1](../jb6Odrq6alA/CARD.md) for the auction and
the four-phase cycle this is the cast of, [video 6](../GGe2widuvPs/CARD.md) for
the same sweep drawn from the order-book side, [video 7](../dAlP8UZXT48/CARD.md)
for structure, and [video 9](../iPDi9_nzn-o/CARD.md) and [video
10](../YwqfGuktXrc/CARD.md) for the two candle-close signals. Read this one for
the motive; read those for the evidence.

## In his words

**Someone is on the other side** *(00:00)*

> Right now, as you read this, someone is on the other side of your trade. Not
> an algorithm, not a computer, a decision made by someone with more capital,
> more information, and more patience than you have right now. And here is the
> uncomfortable truth. They knew you were going to enter before you entered.
> They positioned themselves to take your money. And they did it using your own
> predictable behavior against you. This video is about who that someone
> actually is. Not in a conspiracy theory way, in a mechanical, provable, this
> is how markets actually function way.

**Chess without knowing how the pieces move** *(00:42)*

> There are three types of participants in every financial market. Retail
> traders, institutions, and market makers. Most trading education spends 90%
> of its time teaching you strategies and almost zero time teaching you who you
> are actually trading against. That is like teaching someone how to play chess
> without ever telling them that the pieces move differently. You can memorize
> every opening, but if you do not understand how the other side thinks, you
> will lose every time.

**You are the fuel** *(01:09)*

> Let's start with retail traders, because that is you. And the first thing you
> need to understand about yourself as a retail trader is this. You are not the
> target. You are the fuel. Think about what a retail trader actually is in the
> context of the market. You are someone managing between a few hundred and a
> few hundred thousand dollars. You are using publicly available information.
> Charts, news, YouTube videos, trading courses. You are making decisions based
> on patterns you learned from resources that are available to everyone. And
> you are entering and exiting positions in ways that are almost perfectly
> predictable. Not because you are unintelligent, but because everyone learned
> the same patterns from the same resources.

> **Editor —** The slide behind this puts the retail range at $500 to $500,000.
> Video 1 of the same series draws the same slide with $250,000 at the top; the
> source changed between videos, and neither figure carries any argument.

**Three predictable behaviours** *(01:47)*

> When price approaches a round number, retail traders put their stop losses
> just below it. When price breaks a previous high, retail traders buy the
> breakout. When a hammer candle forms at support, retail traders go long.
> These behaviors are so consistent and so predictable that the participants
> who have the ability to see order flow can literally see your stop losses,
> your entry orders, and your take profit levels sitting in the market before
> you even execute them. This predictability is not a flaw in the system. It is
> the system.

> **Editor —** "Literally see" is too strong for the ES. Stop orders do not
> display in a central limit order book; what a large participant sees is the
> resting limit orders, and what it knows is where retail habitually puts
> stops. Carmine Rosato's [premise episode](../0QlGCz6U_1g/CARD.md) makes the
> same three-behaviour list and the same claim of predictability, and the
> Vorwald podcast's guest, who rejects stop-hunting, still concedes that
> everyone knows where the stops are. Predictable, yes. Visible, no.

**Liquidity, not capital** *(02:19)*

> The retail trader is the most important participant in the market not
> because of the capital they bring, but because of the liquidity they provide.
> Every stop loss you place becomes a buy or sell order the moment it triggers.
> Every breakout entry you take becomes the exit liquidity for someone who
> positioned before the breakout. Your fear and your greed, expressed through
> your trading decisions, create the exact market conditions that larger
> participants need to build and exit their positions efficiently. You are not
> being cheated. The system is working exactly as designed. The question is
> whether you are going to keep being the fuel or learn to read the mechanics
> well enough to stop being used as one.

**Size is the institution's problem** *(03:03)*

> Institutions are not one thing. They are a category that includes central
> banks, commercial banks, hedge funds, sovereign wealth funds, pension funds,
> insurance companies, and proprietary trading firms. What all of these have in
> common is size, and size creates a problem that retail traders never have to
> think about. When you want to buy [...] you click buy and you get filled
> instantly at the market price. Done. When a hedge fund wants to buy $2
> billion [...] the problem is completely different. The moment they start
> buying, the price begins to move against them. Every purchase they make
> drives the price higher, making every subsequent purchase more expensive.
> They are buying against themselves. This is the fundamental mechanical
> constraint that shapes everything institutions do in markets. They cannot
> simply buy when they want to buy and sell when they want to sell. They need
> to engineer the conditions that allow them to fill their orders without
> disturbing the market against themselves.

> **Editor —** The spoken list and the slide's list are both seven long but are
> not the same seven: he says central banks and sovereign wealth funds, the
> slide says investment bank and asset manager. Nothing turns on it. The
> mechanism itself is the one Carmine builds his whole series on, [an
> aggressive order consuming passive liquidity moves price](../QaNPAaEnB5E/CARD.md);
> this is that rule applied to a buyer too large to hide.

**How accumulation works** *(04:07)*

> Here is exactly how institutional accumulation works. An institution
> identifies an asset it wants to own. It does not immediately start buying.
> Instead, it waits. It lets price come to a level where retail selling is
> abundant. A level where retail traders are scared, where momentum is to the
> downside, where everyone believes the price is going lower. This is typically
> after a prolonged decline near a level of obvious support that retail traders
> have their stop losses sitting just below.
>
> Now the institution begins to quietly absorb the retail selling. Every retail
> trader who panics and sells, the institution is on the other side, quietly
> buying. Every stop loss that triggers below support, the institution is
> absorbing those market sell orders. This process takes time. Days, weeks,
> sometimes months. And during this time, price appears to be doing nothing,
> going sideways, boring, unremarkable. This is the most dangerous phase for
> retail traders because boredom causes them to abandon positions right before
> the move begins.

> **Editor —** The slide condenses this to a rule: prolonged decline, plus
> obvious support, plus stops below, equals institutional target. None of the
> three terms is defined here or later in the series. "Quietly absorb" is the
> word Carmine uses too, and his [footprint episode](../eJZhX6Xz4cU/CARD.md) is
> where the corpus shows what absorption looks like on the tape: heavy selling
> printing at a price that will not go lower.

**The final liquidity grab** *(05:13)*

> Once the institution has accumulated enough of its position, something
> specific happens. It engineers a final liquidity grab. Price is pushed below
> that obvious support level, triggering the final cluster of stop losses
> sitting there. Retail traders who were holding on panic and exit. New retail
> traders see the breakdown and short the market. All of this selling pressure
> is absorbed by the institution in a final burst of accumulation. And then,
> with no sellers left, and the institution fully positioned, price reverses
> sharply. The people who shorted the breakdown are now trapped. Their covering
> creates additional buying pressure. The people who were stopped out re-enter
> from the long side, and the institution sitting on a full position built at
> the lowest possible prices watches the markup phase begin. This sequence is
> not theory. It is the documented mechanical behavior of every significant
> market move in history. Wyckoff described it in the 1920s. Smart money
> traders describe it today. The names change. The mechanics do not.

> **Editor —** "Engineers" is the word the [Vorwald podcast](../RwQBdF9TSvc/CARD.md)
> disputes. Its guest agrees that stops get run and positions get closed, and
> denies that anyone is doing it on purpose: an institution absorbing a stop
> cascade would print as size under the low, and what he sees there is
> sometimes two or three contracts, sometimes one, sometimes nothing at all. "Every significant market move in history" is a
> claim no one in the corpus could verify, and the chart under the Wyckoff
> labels is an illustration, not a capture.

**A market maker is a business** *(06:22)*

> Now, let's talk about the market maker, because this is the participant most
> retail traders completely misunderstand. A market maker is not your enemy. A
> market maker is a business. Specifically, it is a business that profits from
> providing liquidity. Here is how it works. When you want to buy [...] someone
> has to be willing to sell it to you at the exact moment you click buy. In a
> perfectly efficient market, you would need to find another trader who wanted
> to sell at the exact same time you wanted to buy. That would make trading
> nearly impossible. The market maker solves this problem. They stand ready to
> buy or sell at any time. They quote a bid price at which they will buy and an
> ask price at which they will sell. The difference between those two prices is
> the spread, and the spread is the market maker's compensation for providing
> instant liquidity. The market maker does not care about direction. They are
> not trying to predict where [...] is going. They are trying to manage a
> balanced book. For every position they take on one side, they are either
> hedging it or waiting for the opposite flow to come in and offset it. Their
> profit comes from the spread, not from directional trading. This is important
> because it means the market maker is simultaneously your broker, your
> counterparty, and a neutral participant who has no opinion about where price
> goes. Understanding this removes a lot of the conspiracy thinking around
> brokers.

> **Editor —** The examples cut here are a spot currency pair, which is where
> "your broker is your counterparty" is true. On the ES it is
> not: the CME is a central limit order book, the clearing house is the
> counterparty to every fill, and the market makers are liquidity providers
> sitting in the same book you trade in. The spread-business description
> survives; the three-roles-one-entity slide does not.

**The unbalanced book** *(07:57)*

> However, there is a nuance that matters. When a large number of retail
> traders are positioned in one direction, the market maker has an unbalanced
> book. They are holding the opposite side of too many trades going one way. To
> rebalance, they need the other side. And the easiest way to create the other
> side is to move price to a level where the opposite flow will naturally
> emerge. This is where the market maker's behavior begins to look suspiciously
> like it is hunting your stop losses. Because in a mechanical sense, it is.
> Not because they hate you, because they need the liquidity your stop losses
> provide to rebalance their own exposure.

> **Editor —** This is the sentence the corpus argues about, stated more
> carefully than anywhere else: yes mechanically, no personally. It is a
> smaller claim than the institutional "engineered grab" above, and it is the
> one [Trading Notes](../IUWvHVout94/CARD.md) also makes before trading the
> sweep. How an unbalanced book shows itself on a chart is never said.

**Predator, prey and environment** *(08:37)*

> Think of the market as a predator and prey ecosystem. The retail trader is
> the most abundant species. They provide the most liquidity and they are the
> most predictable. The institution is the apex predator. It uses the
> predictability of retail behavior to engineer optimal entry and exit
> conditions. The market maker is the environment itself. It provides the
> conditions for the interaction to happen. It profits from the activity
> regardless of who wins or loses. And it occasionally nudges price in
> directions that serve its own need for balanced exposure.

**The patterns that looked random** *(09:10)*

> Once you see the market this way, patterns you previously thought were random
> begin to make sense. Price always seems to sweep just below your stop before
> reversing. That is the institution and market maker creating the conditions
> for accumulation while eliminating the retail traders who would provide
> resistance to the next move. Price always seems to break out just as you
> finally buy the resistance level. That is institutional distribution into
> your buying. The breakout you bought is their exit. The rally you missed by
> two candles before you entered is the markup phase that has already been
> fully funded by the accumulation you were not watching.

> **Editor —** "The breakout you bought is their exit" is Carmine's [fade the
> first test of a breakout](../w7tvJCuZAq8/CARD.md), and his wholesale-versus-
> market-price framing in the [premise episode](../0QlGCz6U_1g/CARD.md) says the
> same thing about the same trade. The two schools disagree about tools and
> vocabulary; on this they are one voice.

**Reading the evidence, not predicting** *(09:50)*

> The question you should be asking at this point is not how do I get lucky
> enough to be on the right side. The question is how do I read the evidence
> that tells me what the institution is doing before it becomes obvious. And
> the answer to that question is exactly what the rest of this series teaches.
> Market structure tells you the direction the institution has chosen.
> Liquidity zones tell you where the institution is likely to engineer its next
> move. Order blocks tell you where the institutional buying or selling
> originated. Volume analysis tells you whether the move you are seeing is
> institutional conviction or retail noise. Fair value gaps tell you where the
> institution moved price so quickly that it created an imbalance it will
> likely return to fill. Every concept in this series is a tool for reading
> institutional behavior. Not for predicting the future. For reading the
> evidence of decisions that have already been made by participants with the
> information advantage.

> **Editor —** Of the five tools, the series delivers structure in full
> ([video 7](../dAlP8UZXT48/CARD.md), [video 9](../iPDi9_nzn-o/CARD.md),
> [video 10](../YwqfGuktXrc/CARD.md)) and the order-book mechanics in
> [video 6](../GGe2widuvPs/CARD.md). Order blocks and fair value gaps are
> defined in passing; volume never gets its own video in the ten we hold.

**Read the need** *(10:47)*

> Here is the mindset shift. Stop thinking about your trade as you versus the
> market. The market is not a single opponent. It is an ecosystem of
> participants with different sizes, different information, and different
> goals. Your job is not to beat the market. Your job is to identify which
> participant is currently in control, what that participant needs to happen
> next, and whether the evidence on your chart supports the conclusion that
> what that participant needs is about to happen. When institution needs to
> accumulate, price will move to create panic selling. When institution needs
> to distribute, price will move to create FOMO buying. When market maker needs
> to rebalance, price will sweep to collect the stop losses that create the
> opposite flow. Read the need. Position for the consequence. That is
> institutional trading in one sentence.
