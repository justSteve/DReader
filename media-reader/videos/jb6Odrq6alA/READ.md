# Read: How Markets Actually Work — Retail vs Institutions vs Market Makers Explained (Video 1)

> **The smart-money school's first lesson, and it is the same as Carmine Rosato's: price moves because one side is more aggressive, not because there are more buyers. Three kinds of participant turn that dial, and their interaction draws the accumulation, markup, distribution, markdown cycle every later video in the series is a tool for reading.**

- **Channel:** Smart Money Decode X · Zero to Advanced Trader, video 1 · **Uploaded:** 2026-08-01 · **Duration:** 16:31
- **Card:** [CARD.md](CARD.md) · **Watch:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jb6Odrq6alA

## In brief

Video one of a ten-part beginner series: whiteboard slides, a narrator, no
presenter, no live chart and no trade. Its claim is that a market is a
continuous auction, not a store. The price is whatever the last transaction
printed at, and the next print is higher or lower depending on who initiated
it. "Price does not go up because there are more buyers than sellers"; every
trade has exactly one buyer and one seller, so what moves price is the
aggression of the side that is initiating.

Three classes of participant turn that dial. Retail is small, reactive and fed
on public information. Institutions have one problem, size: a large order moves
the price against itself, so they accumulate slowly and in disguise. Market
makers quote both sides and live on the spread. Their interaction produces the
four-phase cycle the rest of the series learns to read. Nothing here is a
rule; every number on screen is an illustration.

## The argument

The narrator opens on a distinction Carmine Rosato's
[foundation episode](../QaNPAaEnB5E/CARD.md) also opens on, nearly word for
word. A store has a seller who sets a price and a buyer who accepts or leaves.
A market is an auction in which every participant is a potential buyer and
seller at once, and the price is only the last transaction. From there the
"one idea that changes everything": more buyers than sellers is not why price
rises, because a completed trade always has one of each. What rises price is
buyers who will pay up to be filled, lifting the offer; what drops it is
sellers who will take less to get out, hitting the bid. Where the schools
part is on where to see it. Carmine reads
aggression on a footprint and an order book; this video reads it off the
candle body, a fast rise being buyers urgently lifting offers, and grades
bodies on an aggression scale. Carmine's
[footprint episode](../eJZhX6Xz4cU/CARD.md) exists to say that the candle is
the wrong place to look for exactly this. The series reaches the book in [video 6](../GGe2widuvPs/CARD.md), where a
market order consuming a resting limit order is the mechanism of a price
change.

The institutional section carries the one piece of reasoning a trader can
use. An institution cannot click buy on a
billion-dollar order and get one price; filling it moves the market against
the filler before it is done. Size is therefore a constraint, and the
constraint forces slow, disguised accumulation over a range, which is where the
patterns come from. That figure is a cartoon; the corpus's concrete
calibration of "large" on the S&P is the Vorwald channel's
[over 250 to 300 ES contracts](../Vd83oo_geMk/CARD.md) for a standalone order,
and the [325-contract ask](../vl01TiVTuoQ/CARD.md) the market ran to in its
live session. The [Vorwald podcast](../RwQBdF9TSvc/CARD.md) agrees with the
constraint half entirely: a large position is built over a day or longer,
sometimes weeks, inside a range where prices are fair. That is this video's
accumulation zone under another name.

Market makers get the spread, and a warning that your broker may be on the
other side of your trade. That is a picture of a CFD or dealing-desk broker.
On ES the counterparty is whoever rests on the exchange's book; the market
maker is one participant on it, not your dealer.

The cycle is where the corpus splits, and this video's own text shows the
seam. In the narration the accumulating institution "may allow price to dip
slightly, even helping create panic in retail traders who then sell cheaply".
The slide over it reads "engineered dip". The hedge is spoken; the certainty
is drawn. Carmine's [premise episode](../0QlGCz6U_1g/CARD.md) tells the same
story without the hedge: patient money buys from stopped-out sellers at
wholesale inside the stop hunt and sells to the chasers at market price, and
he trades it that way. The Vorwald podcast rejects the agency, not the
mechanics: the trades printing under chart lows are single-digit, so no
institution is fishing there, while conceding that positions do have to be
closed when a level goes. The series' own [video 3](../7F9wBYse-Nk/CARD.md)
lands between them, "hunting your stops? Yes, mechanically", and
[Trading Notes](../IUWvHVout94/CARD.md) puts "nobody is hunting your stops" on
screen and then trades the sweep. Video 1 sits nearest Carmine: the
institution helps create the panic, and later "is using their FOMO buying as
liquidity to exit". Its closing scoreboard, most money, most information and most patience wins,
is Carmine's Buffett line about impatient and patient money in a different
hand.

The chart walkthrough gives the cycle its tells: a range where retail gets
bored, a breakout on expanding volume and a decisive candle that begins
markup, then new highs on smaller candles with erratic volume as the warning
that smart money is leaving, then a markdown faster than the rise. Two
cautions. Carmine's [volume episode](../w7tvJCuZAq8/CARD.md) would fade the
first test of that breakout and buy only the pullback that absorbs the
selling, and the series itself, by [video 10](../YwqfGuktXrc/CARD.md), enters
the retest rather than the break candle, so the disagreement is smaller than
the slide suggests. And the "actual chart" is an AI-generated mock-up with
incoherent axes; only the drawn annotations are content.

What the video never supplies is a measurable rule: what counts as aggressive,
how long accumulation lasts, what erratic volume means. The
[series synthesis](../../playlists/smdx-zero-to-advanced-PLU9kIorYkc18.md)
finds the same absence across all ten videos. Read this one for the frame,
and for the fact that two schools that disagree downstream begin from the same
sentence.

## In his words

*The narrator is unseen; the channel shows no presenter. Passages are trimmed
to the teaching. The video's examples are worked on other instruments and are
elided here; the theory does not depend on them.*

**Not a store, an auction** *(02:40)*

> "A financial market is not a store. In a store, there is a seller and there
> is a buyer. The seller sets the price. The buyer decides whether to accept
> it. Simple, one way. But a financial market is an auction, a continuous
> real-time auction where every participant is simultaneously a potential
> buyer and a potential seller, and the price is always the result of their
> collective negotiation. There is no single seller setting a fixed price. The
> price is whatever the most recent transaction happened at. And the next
> price will be higher or lower depending on whether the next transaction was
> initiated by someone who wanted to buy or someone who wanted to sell."

> **Editor —** Carmine's [first episode](../QaNPAaEnB5E/CARD.md) makes the
> same move with the same two words, store and auction. The two schools share
> their premise and almost nothing after it.

**The one idea** *(03:27)*

> "Here is the one idea that changes everything about how you understand
> markets. Price does not go up because there are more buyers than sellers.
> And price does not go down because there are more sellers than buyers. This
> is one of the most common misconceptions in all of trading. And it is taught
> everywhere. The truth is mathematically simple. Every single trade requires
> exactly one buyer and exactly one seller simultaneously. [...] The number of
> buyers always equals the number of sellers in any completed transaction.
> What actually moves price is not the quantity of buyers versus sellers. It
> is the aggression of the side that is initiating.
>
> When buyers are aggressive, they're willing to pay higher prices to get
> filled. They are lifting the offer, accepting whatever price is available.
> Their aggression drives price up. When sellers are aggressive, they are
> willing to accept lower prices to get out. They are hitting the bid,
> accepting whatever price is available. Their aggression drives price down.
> Price movement is about which side is more desperate, more motivated, more
> urgent, not which side has more people."

**The painting** *(04:51)*

> "Imagine two people at an auction for a painting. One person wants the
> painting and will pay any price to get it. The other person has the painting
> and is happy to hold it unless someone offers a very good price. That
> dynamic, one side aggressive, one side passive, is what determines where
> price goes.
>
> In financial markets, when you see price rising quickly, it means buyers are
> urgently lifting offers, accepting higher and higher prices because they
> believe the value will be even higher soon. When you see price falling
> quickly, it means sellers are urgently hitting bids, accepting lower and
> lower prices because they believe the value will be even lower soon. The
> speed and conviction of that aggression is what you see on a chart as the
> size and character of each candle."

> **Editor —** The last sentence is the fork in the road. Carmine's
> [footprint episode](../eJZhX6Xz4cU/CARD.md) argues that a candle body hides
> the one thing worth knowing, aggression that fails to move price, and that
> you need the transactions per price to see it. This series reads the body
> and grades it high, medium or no clear aggression. Neither side gives a
> threshold.

**Retail** *(06:18)*

> "Retail traders are individuals trading their own money, typically through
> a broker platform in relatively small sizes. A retail trader might be
> managing a few hundred dollars or a few hundred thousand dollars. In the
> context of a global financial market that trades trillions of dollars per
> day, even a relatively wealthy retail trader is an extremely small
> participant. Retail traders use publicly available information, technical
> analysis, trading courses, YouTube channels, forums, and their own
> experience to make decisions. They are reactive. They respond to price
> movement rather than creating it."

**Size is the institution's problem** *(07:19)*

> "These participants manage billions of dollars, and their individual trades
> are large enough to actually move markets. An institutional trader placing
> a billion-dollar order cannot simply click buy and get filled at one price.
> Their order is so large that filling it all at once would move the price
> against them before they finish. This is a critical point. Institutions
> cannot trade freely because their size is their problem. They need to
> disguise their activity. They need to accumulate positions slowly over time
> in ways that do not alert the market to what they are doing. And the way
> they do this creates the patterns you will see on charts."

> **Editor —** The billion is a cartoon, and the slide's ladder of purchases
> at rising cost is a cartoon of slippage. For what "large" looks like on the
> S&P, the corpus has the Vorwald channel's
> [over 250 to 300 contracts](../Vd83oo_geMk/CARD.md) for a single standalone
> order. The [Vorwald podcast](../RwQBdF9TSvc/CARD.md) describes the same
> constraint from a desk: a position built over a day or weeks, inside a range
> where prices are fair. Everyone in the corpus agrees size must hide. They
> disagree about what it does while hiding, which is the next passage but
> two.

**The spread** *(08:10)*

> "Market makers are entities that exist specifically to provide liquidity to
> the market. They quote both a buy price and a sell price simultaneously and
> they profit from the difference between those two prices, which is called
> the spread. Market makers are not trying to predict direction. They are
> trying to manage inventory, balancing their buy and sell exposure while
> collecting the spread on every transaction. When you open a trade with your
> broker, in many cases you are trading against the broker's own market
> making desk or a connected liquidity provider. Understanding this is
> important because it means your broker has interests that may not always be
> perfectly aligned with yours."

> **Editor —** True of a dealing-desk broker; not how ES works, where your
> order meets the exchange's book and the broker is a conduit. One curiosity
> from the card: the slide's own four-transaction spread ladder is
> inconsistent at its third step, and Gemini transcribed the version the
> pattern implied rather than the one printed. Frames caught it. The lesson is
> about the slide, not the theory.

**Accumulation** *(09:24)*

> "At this price, some participants believe [it] is cheap and want to buy
> more. Others believe it is expensive and want to sell. Others are neutral
> and waiting to see what happens. This is the market in balance. The price
> represents the current consensus about value.
>
> Now, imagine a large institution decides it wants to accumulate a
> significant position [...] because it believes the price will be
> significantly higher in the future. It cannot simply place a massive buy
> order because that would immediately push the price up, meaning it would be
> buying its own position more expensively. Instead, it begins to accumulate
> quietly. It buys in small amounts across many different prices. It may
> allow price to dip slightly, even helping create panic in retail traders
> who then sell [...] cheaply, giving the institution more inventory at lower
> prices. This slow, patient accumulation is what creates the sideways
> consolidation zones you see on charts before major moves. The price is not
> going nowhere. It is being slowly loaded with institutional buy orders."

> **Editor —** "May allow price to dip slightly, even helping create panic"
> is the whole stop-hunt debate in one hedged clause. The slide over it says
> "engineered dip". Carmine's [premise episode](../0QlGCz6U_1g/CARD.md)
> drops the hedge: the dip under the level is where patient money buys at
> wholesale, and he buys there. The [Vorwald podcast](../RwQBdF9TSvc/CARD.md)
> keeps the mechanics and rejects the author: the prints under a low are
> single-digit, so nobody with size is down there engineering anything, though
> positions do get closed when a level goes. This series' own
> [video 3](../7F9wBYse-Nk/CARD.md) says yes, mechanically. The narration
> here is closer to the podcast than its own slide is.

**Markup** *(10:38)*

> "Once the institution has accumulated enough of its position, the dynamic
> changes. The supply of willing sellers at current prices starts to dry up
> because the institution has absorbed most of them. With sellers becoming
> scarce, any new buy order has to go to a higher price to find a willing
> seller. Price begins to rise. More participants notice the rising price and
> want to buy, adding their own demand. The momentum builds. The institution,
> which accumulated at lower prices, is now watching its position become more
> profitable with each tick higher. This is markup."

> **Editor —** "Absorbed most of them" is Carmine's word too, and the one
> order-flow mechanism both schools describe identically: passive size
> soaking up aggressive selling until there is none left, then price has to
> travel to find the next seller.

**Distribution and markdown** *(11:25)*

> "Eventually, price reaches a level where the institution wants to exit its
> position. It has made its profit and wants to lock it in. But, it faces the
> same problem in reverse. It cannot simply sell everything at once because
> that would crash the price before it finishes selling. So, it distributes
> its position slowly, selling into the demand created by retail traders who
> are now excited about the price rise and buying aggressively because they
> fear missing out. The institution is using their FOMO buying as liquidity
> to exit. Once the institution has sold its position, the support that was
> holding price up disappears. There are no more large buyers absorbing the
> selling. Price begins to fall. Retail traders who bought near the top are
> now sitting on losses and eventually panic, adding selling pressure. This
> is markdown. The phase where the smart money has exited and the uninformed
> money is left holding positions that will continue to fall."

**The heartbeat** *(12:33)*

> "This four-phase cycle, accumulation, markup, distribution, markdown,
> repeats endlessly across every financial market at every time frame. It is
> the heartbeat of every chart you will ever look at and every concept this
> series teaches you from liquidity pools to order blocks to volume analysis
> to price action patterns is a tool for identifying where in the cycle the
> market currently is and positioning yourself accordingly."

**Reading it on a chart** *(13:10)*

> "A period of sideways movement where price oscillates in a range without
> clear direction. This is accumulation. Large players are loading their
> positions. Retail traders are bored and confused. Then a breakout occurs
> with expanding volume and large decisive candles. This is the beginning of
> markup. Trend followers and momentum traders join. Then a period near the
> highs where price makes new highs, but the candles become smaller, volume
> becomes erratic, and progress slows. This is distribution. Smart money is
> exiting. Finally, a sharp decline, often faster than the rise with momentum
> and panic. This is markdown. And somewhere at the end of that markdown,
> accumulation begins again."

> **Editor —** The narration calls this "an actual chart". It is not: every
> chart in the video is an AI-generated pastiche of a charting platform, with
> price axes that do not run in order. Only the drawn labels are content.
> On the breakout itself, Carmine's [volume episode](../w7tvJCuZAq8/CARD.md)
> would fade its first test and buy the pullback that absorbs the selling,
> never the break; this series, by [video 10](../YwqfGuktXrc/CARD.md), also
> enters the retest rather than the break candle. "Expanding volume" and
> "erratic volume" are never given a number here or anywhere in the series.

**Traces** *(14:02)*

> "The market is not random. It does not move because of luck or because
> charts have magical patterns. It moves because of the decisions of
> participants with different sizes, different information, and different
> time horizons. And those decisions leave traces on a chart. Every candle
> tells part of the story. Every volume bar adds evidence. Every sweep of a
> swing high or low has a reason. The entire purpose of this series is to
> teach you how to read those traces. How to identify who is doing what and
> why. How to stop being the retail trader who provides liquidity to
> institutions and start being the trader who positions alongside
> institutional activity before it becomes obvious to everyone else."

> **Editor —** "Every sweep has a reason" is what he says; the slide names
> the reason, "stop collection". As with the engineered dip, the drawing
> commits where the voice does not. The candle-as-record idea is
> [video 4](../oktlv1rOG9Q/CARD.md)'s whole subject.

**A competition, not a puzzle** *(15:00)*

> "Most beginners approach trading thinking the market is a puzzle to be
> solved. Find the right indicator, find the right pattern, find the magic
> formula. The market is not a puzzle. It is a competition. And in this
> competition, the participants with the most money, the most information,
> and the most patience consistently take money from the participants with
> the least of all three. Your job is not to find a secret formula. Your job
> is to understand the competition well enough to avoid being the one who
> loses, and eventually to position yourself on the same side as the
> participants who consistently win."

> **Editor —** Compare Carmine's [premise episode](../0QlGCz6U_1g/CARD.md),
> which throws out "retail versus institution" for Buffett's line about money
> moving from the impatient to the patient. Same scoreboard, and the same
> gap after it: neither video says how patience is measured or what it costs
> in missed trades.
