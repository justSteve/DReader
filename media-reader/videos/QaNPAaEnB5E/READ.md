# Read: The only trading strategy you need to use if you're starting out...

> **Carmine Rosato's foundation episode: every market is an auction, price moves only when an aggressive order hits a passive one, and reading which side is which is the whole edge.**

- **Channel:** Carmine Rosato (Jumpstart Trading) · **Uploaded:** 2024-10-14 · **Duration:** 21:47
- **Card:** [CARD.md](CARD.md) · **Watch:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QaNPAaEnB5E

## In brief

Episode one of eight, and despite the title there is no strategy in it. What
there is instead is the premise every later episode stands on. Carmine Rosato
argues that a stock chart is no different from a price history of cars or
houses: every market is an auction, and the same two things happen in all of
them. Buyers and sellers agree on a range, which he calls fair value or
balance, and then something makes one side pay up or give way, an imbalance,
until a new range forms.

Underneath that sits the mechanical claim that carries the series. Every
transaction has exactly one buyer and one seller, so there are never "more
buyers than sellers"; what varies is who was passive, resting on a limit
order, and who was aggressive, hitting it with a market order. Reading that
disequilibrium, he says, is the strategy. Indicators and ICT are upstream
noise: for price to move, an order has to be placed.

## The argument

He builds the episode out of things that are not stocks: cars, a house, a
trading pit, and only at the end a chart. "All a chart does is portray where
the market has currently traded and where it was trading in the past." The pit
is his origin story: whoever shouted loudest got filled, and "our pit is the
candlestick charts behind the screens".

The first model is balance and imbalance, told through a car. Dealers and
purchasers agree the car is worth $35,000 and trade there for months: fair
value. Then it is listed at $40,000, and the only question is whether
purchasers will pay. If they do, fair value is now $40,000; if not, dealers
are forced back down. The slide adds a leg he does not narrate, a sell
imbalance to a new fair value at $36,500. Every school in the corpus shares
the premise. What Carmine never gives is a proportion or a boundary. The
Vorwald channel supplies both: balance roughly 70% of the time, and the value
area high and low defined as where the market starts moving into imbalance.

The second model is acceptance and rejection, told through a house asked at
$400,000 and bid at $300,000. From it he gets continuation and reversal: an
external force pushes price out of the range, and the market trends if the new
prices are accepted and reverses if they are not. One line outweighs the rest:
"the market moves up until the last buyer buys". That is the seed of his own
volume tail in episodes 2 and 5 and of what the Vorwald channel's Market
Profile videos call a buying or selling tail. The Vorwald channel also
attaches the target Carmine leaves out: price that re-enters a previous value
area and finds acceptance has a high probability of traversing the full
profile. Where the schools part is how acceptance is read. The Vorwald channel
and SMDX read it on candle closes; Carmine, by episode 7, insists on the live
tape and never a close. Here he has not yet said either.

Then the mechanical core. A limit order is passive: a buyer resting on the
bid, a seller on the ask, adding liquidity, "letting the market come to you".
A market order is aggressive: it hits the resting order, takes the liquidity
and completes the transaction. So every transaction has one buyer and one
seller, "there can never be more buyers than sellers", and what is unequal is
passive against aggressive. Reading that "is where the strategy comes in".
Three schools that never cite each other arrive at this sentence: SMDX's first
video states it as "AGGRESSION MOVES PRICE, NOT QUANTITY"; the Vorwald channel
defines order flow as "who is currently buying, who is currently selling, and
how aggressively". The split is also the embryo of absorption, aggressive
orders failing to move price because a passive counterparty is taking them,
which episodes 2 and 5 make a setup and the Vorwald channel reaches
independently.

The chart demonstration is the only setup content. Price rises into a level
with a lack of buying volume and a lot of sellers, so buyers will not pay up
and sellers must lower price to find trade: short. Price sells into demand,
buying steps in, buyers bid successively higher: continuation. There is no
threshold on "a lack of buying volume", and none arrives in eight episodes.
The only calibration in the corpus is the Vorwald channel's: an ES resting
order above roughly 250 to 300 contracts counts as truly large, at least 200
to 300 trades before statistics mean anything, and a worked example at a 50%
hit rate giving an average risk-reward of "maybe a two".

The funnel is where he places every other school. Indicators at the top, ICT
and Smart Money Concepts under them, algorithms and Fibonacci below, and out
of the spout an order. He does not reject ICT; he subordinates it, a distinct
third position. SMDX is built on the sweep-then-change-of-character sequence;
the Vorwald channel calls stop-hunting a retail myth, with single-digit
contracts printing below the lows where the hunters should be. Carmine says
all of it is upstream of the order, and in episode 4 he uses stop hunts
himself, as a place institutions buy at wholesale.

Two honest notes. The title promises "the only day trading strategy you would
ever need" and the episode contains a premise, not a strategy; the parameters
that would make it one never arrive in the series. And his pit, where "all
participants had an equal shot" and retail "can compete with institutional
traders", sits awkwardly beside episode 4, where retail is the bait fish.
Episode 3 will make the Bookmap heatmap a core tool; the Vorwald channel says
professionals do not use one.

## In his words

**Every market is an auction** *(01:04)*

> "The first thing that we have to understand is the stock market is just like
> any other marketplace, because all marketplaces are auctions where buyers or
> sellers are looking to transact and fulfill their needs. Normal traders are
> used to a candlestick chart representing the market. If we were to chart the
> prices of real estate, cars, watches, we can formulate a similar candlestick
> chart, because all a chart does is portray where the market has currently
> traded and where it was trading in the past. So there's no difference
> between the stock market and any other auction."

**The pit** *(02:05)*

> "Back in the 1980s, early '90s, before computers were a big thing, you had
> essentially a bunch of traders trading in the pits for their specific
> marketplace. You would have hundreds of guys and girls throwing up their
> hands, shouting, yelling whether they want to get in or out of a position.
> They would fight, they would push, they would curse out each other, because
> whoever is the loudest, whoever shows they are looking to buy or sell, would
> then get fulfilled. To profit is their end goal."

> "Our pit is the candlestick charts behind the screens. There's no difference
> now in 2024 than there is in the 1970s, other than technology and other than
> computers." *(03:24)*

> "This open outcry system, back in the pit trading days, all participants had
> an equal shot, that the market was transparent, efficient and fair price
> discovery to complete transactions. Now behind the computer screens, where
> us retail traders can compete with other institutional traders to profit
> with this increased participation in the market. And this is the component
> to the strategy: is reading the participation in the market." *(03:51)*

> **Editor —** "An equal shot" and "retail can compete" are the rosy version.
> Three episodes later he tells the same audience that retail is the bait fish
> and institutions need it to sell so they can buy. Both can be true of an
> auction; he never reconciles them, and the later, harder view is the one his
> trades actually use.

**Fair value, told through a car** *(04:51)*

> "In a fair market, let's just say the fair value for a specific vehicle is
> $35,000. In every market there are two participants. In this example we
> could say that there's a dealer, someone who owns that car, and the
> purchaser, who is looking to purchase that car. In the stock market we can
> say that a purchaser is someone who is looking to buy or sell a specific
> stock or future instrument, and the dealer could be a market maker making
> that market and giving out the spreads or controlling the volume and the
> shares of that specific instrument."

> "We've been looking for the past 3 months and we've seen the value of the
> specific car we're looking at has been between 35 and $37,000 for 3 months.
> I haven't really seen any extremes of this, and this has been the fair value
> of the price of that car. As a purchaser I am comfortable paying $35,
> $37,000, because this is what the market has been. Now as a dealer, if I'm
> looking to clear off my lot, and I know the market finds fair value at
> $35,000, then I'm willing to sell the car to whoever is willing to buy it
> around $35,000. This is fair value, where both the dealer and the purchaser
> will make out on the deal." *(05:31)*

**Imbalance** *(06:16)*

> "Now there will be a point where the market has to then discover new prices.
> This is where a price imbalance forms. Now let's say 4 months later we see a
> car that gets listed, the same car, and it gets listed for $40,000. Then all
> of a sudden, a few days later, we see every other dealer that has stock of
> this car is selling at between $40 to $42,000. And now, as the dealers are
> willing to sell here at $40,000, are the purchasers, this is the question
> mark, are the purchasers willing to spend $40,000? If they are willing to
> spend $40,000, then the market will find fair value up here at 40,000,
> because you have both the purchaser willing to pay for it and the dealer
> willing to sell at this $40,000. Now the market has shifted. We've created
> this imbalance from 35,000 to 40,000, that now the market has found fair
> value where both buyers and sellers are in agreement at that specific
> price."

> "Now if the purchaser was not willing to buy at $40,000, and the dealer is
> trying but no buyer is willing to pay $40,000 from that dealer, well then
> that dealer will then be forced to lower its price. And this is where a sell
> imbalance comes: where the buyers are not willing to pay these high prices,
> and that forces the dealer to then lower their price so that they can
> complete the transaction. And the market will then move down in a sell
> imbalance to fulfill the need of those purchasers and then find fair value
> again. So the market constantly moves from fair value to unfair value, from
> balance to imbalance. This is how a market works, whether we're talking
> about cars, stocks, futures, options, crypto. This is how an auction
> facilitates its trade." *(07:24)*

> **Editor —** The slide behind him draws the full cycle: $35,000, a buy
> imbalance to $40,000, then a sell imbalance he never narrates down to a new
> fair value at $36,500. What the slide and the speech both lack is any sense
> of proportion. The Vorwald channel puts the market in balance roughly 70% of
> the time and defines the value area high and low as the prices where the
> market starts moving into imbalance. That is the same picture with edges you
> can locate on a profile.

**Acceptance, told through a house** *(08:27)*

> "Put yourself in the position of you are looking to purchase a home. You see
> a beautiful house on the market, it just got listed, and the seller listed
> their house, or they offered it, or their asking price. Two key words there:
> they're offering, and their asking price is $400,000. This is what they are
> willing to sell their house for. Now as a buyer, you really like their
> house, but you're not willing to pay $400,000 for the house. You're willing
> to pay a hundred, you're willing to pay 200, but now you put out a bid for
> $300,000. You say, okay, this is the most amount of money I'm willing to
> pay. The market moves up until the last buyer buys."

> "If no buyer is willing to buy at $400,000 for this house, then the only
> option for the market to work would be for this seller at $400,000 to lower
> their price. And this is going to be relatable to the stock market moving
> down, or you see a very large red candle to the downside, because the
> sellers are essentially forced to lower their prices to fulfill the need of
> buyers, because the buyers are willing to pay a certain maximum price, and
> in this example it's $300,000. So this seller had their house listed for 5
> months and no one is willing to buy it. The seller's getting frustrated, the
> seller's ready to give up, the seller is ready to panic sell, or drop or
> lower the price, to basically find a buyer who is willing to pay a
> comfortable price at a much lower discount. In this example, call it
> $300,000." *(09:10)*

> **Editor —** "The market moves up until the last buyer buys" is the most
> useful sentence in the episode, and he walks past it. It is the volume tail
> he teaches in episodes 2 and 5, where prints thin to nothing at an extreme
> because the last buyer has bought, and it is what the Vorwald channel's
> Market Profile videos call a buying or selling tail. Three sources, one
> observation, no shared vocabulary.

**Continuation and reversal** *(10:25)*

> "The next concept of the market is the market's going to have continuation
> moves, this is where we get the imbalance, and the market's going to have
> reversal moves. Well, when the market finds a period of fair value where
> buyers and sellers are willing to sell and buy at that range, when an
> external force comes and they find acceptance of those prices, then the
> market will move up. The market, once it finds unacceptance or a rejection,
> this is when you were going to find the reversal, like I just stated about
> the house being listed for so long and no one was willing to bite, so the
> seller was forced to drop the price. This information in the stock market is
> an edge, where we can look to play reversals, versus an example where we
> look to play continuation."

> "This is how the market continues to auction and continues to trend: once
> value has been accepted. If value is not accepted, then the market will
> reverse." *(11:49)*

> **Editor —** Acceptance is the hinge of every school in this corpus, and
> they disagree about how to see it. The Vorwald channel reads it on candle
> closes and gives it a payoff Carmine never does: price that re-enters a
> prior value area and finds acceptance has a high probability of traversing
> the whole profile. SMDX also reads closes. Carmine, by episode 7, will
> insist acceptance is read on the live tape and never on a close, because
> waiting for the close moves the stop. In this episode he has not chosen yet.

**Passive means limit** *(12:00)*

> "I'm sure a lot of you have heard market versus limit orders. What's more
> significant is understanding how each order affects the market, because
> being able to read if a limit or a market order comes in will give us an
> advantage. Briefly, a limit order. You're going to hear me use the word
> passive a lot. Passive, when I refer to that, essentially means a limit
> order. And when I place a limit order, I'm placing a buy or sell order at a
> specific price or better. I'm looking to sell my house at $400,000. $400,000
> is the minimum I am willing to sell my house for. I have a limit order
> there: I'm offering, or I am asking, to sell at $400,000 or more."

> "Now a passive buyer buys on the bid. You want to buy Apple stock, but
> you're only willing to pay $100 for Apple stock. Well, you can put a limit
> order at $100, and that order will then be placed on the bid. This adds
> liquidity to the market. When you place a limit order, I don't really want
> to say you're a dealer, but you're adding liquidity to the market, because
> you're putting an order on the book, which then adds liquidity, because you
> are looking to buy or sell at that specific price. This essentially is you
> being very patient, and you're letting the market come to you." *(12:53)*

**Aggressive means market** *(13:38)*

> "You're willing to pay $300,000 for the house. Well, guess what, the seller
> comes in and the seller says, hey, I see that you just put a bid on my house
> for $300,000. I was foolish for asking $400,000. I think $300,000 is a
> better price. I'm going to accept your bid of $300,000. That seller in that
> example essentially is taking the liquidity away from the market. That
> seller has basically completed a transaction because they were aggressive.
> They couldn't sell their house for $400,000, so they had to be aggressive
> and accept the bid for that buyer that came in. This, in the stock market,
> is a market order. It's taking liquidity away from that bid that was placed
> there, and you're hitting that passive buyer, and this then completes a
> transaction."

**The sentence the series rests on** *(14:39)*

> "A passive buyer buys on the bid, and that transaction could only be
> completed if an aggressive seller then market orders and hits the bid. A
> passive seller limits on the ask, and his limit order, for whatever asking
> price he was looking to sell that good for, will only be completed, or he
> will only be fulfilled, if a buyer comes in and says, hey, I'm willing to
> accept your asking price. There will always be a buyer and a seller for
> every transaction. There can never be more buyers than sellers. There can
> never be more sellers than buyers. This is crucial, because a lot of people
> say, hey, there's more buyers here, hey, there's more sellers here. It's
> impossible. For every transaction there is an equilibrium between buyers and
> sellers. There will be a disequilibrium between passive participants and
> aggressive participants, and this is where the strategy comes in: is
> figuring out the difference between passive and aggressive participants and
> seeing how the market reacts and responds to this. This is how the strategy
> is built, and this is where the edge comes to profit in the market."

> **Editor —** Three schools that do not cite each other say this. Smart Money
> Decode X opens its series with "AGGRESSION MOVES PRICE, NOT QUANTITY" and
> later walks a market order into a resting sell to show the fill is the new
> price. The Vorwald channel defines order flow as who is buying, who is
> selling, and how aggressively. What Carmine has not said yet, and will in
> episodes 2 and 5, is the corollary that makes it tradeable: when aggressive
> orders keep arriving and price does not move, a passive party is absorbing
> them. The Vorwald channel arrives at the same read on its own.

**The chart** *(17:02)*

> "Think about this. If the market's coming up to this level of resistance and
> we see a lack of buying volume, and we see a lot of sellers here, but the
> buyers are not willing to pay this price, well then guess what, we can look
> to capitalize and profit on a reversal setup at this level of resistance, or
> at these prices, because we know buyers are not willing to pay the price,
> and sellers, if they want to profit, are going to be forced to lower the
> price. And we can capitalize on this information and look to take the market
> short, to play a bearish position, because we know the chances of the market
> moving down to facilitate trade lower, to fulfill the needs of those buyers,
> will be very high."

> "Or if the market's selling off into this level of demand, at a level of
> support, if we see a lot of buying activity stepping in down here, and
> buyers are willing to pay these lower prices, and sellers are willing to
> also sell to these buyers at these lower prices, well then guess what the
> market then does after. And if buyers are willing to pay higher prices and
> they're bidding and bidding and bidding, well guess what, that's when we're
> going to get the trending market to the upside, and we're going to get these
> continuation type of moves." *(17:39)*

> **Editor —** This is the whole of the setup content, and it has no
> threshold. How little buying volume is "a lack"? How many sellers are "a
> lot"? He never says, here or in any later episode. The only calibration in
> the corpus is the Vorwald channel's, and it is for a different object: an ES
> resting order above roughly 250 to 300 contracts counts as truly large. Two
> notes from the card on the chart itself: the lower zone he narrates as
> support carries the label RESISTANCE, and that support bounce sits to the
> left of the rally into the upper zone, so he is narrating the chart out of
> order. Neither changes the lesson; both say he reads participants, not
> labels.

**What the market is for** *(18:23)*

> "You have to agree and understand that the market is an auction place, and
> its main objective is just to advertise the prices and to see if
> participants agree or disagree with those prices, because the market's main
> objective is to facilitate trade. Once you understand this, this is the best
> strategy to use, and we can formulate it and build an edge around this. It's
> not off of these moving averages, it's not off of all these indicators that
> all these traders use. Yes, I'm not going to disagree that you cannot form a
> strategy off of this, you can easily form a strategy, but these indicators
> is not the reason why price moves. These are tools to represent how or where
> price is. The market doesn't move because the RSI is oversold or overbought.
> The market doesn't move because we just rejected your moving average."

**The funnel** *(19:12)*

> "Think about this in terms of a funnel. We have a funnel, we got water going
> into the funnel. The water at some point will come and flow out the bottom
> of the funnel. At the top of the funnel, let's just call it indicators,
> moving averages, RSI or any other trading tool. Under it we have ICT, we
> have Smart Money Concepts, and then under that we have any algorithmic
> trading or any other trading strategy, Fibonacci, any strategy that you want
> to call or use. Regardless of the strategy, regardless of the tool, an order
> has to be placed. So at the end of the funnel a buy or sell order is placed.
> Let's say you see the RSI is oversold. Well yes, that information may cause
> other people to buy or sell. That buy or sell order is at the bottom of the
> funnel, because regardless of what causes people to buy or sell, an order
> has to be placed for the market to move. So being able to read the orders
> essentially avoids all the other noise at the top of the funnel, and you
> want to focus on what is causing the market to move: the water at the bottom
> of the funnel, and that is a buy or sell order."

> **Editor —** Note where ICT lands: inside the funnel, one input among many,
> neither endorsed nor dismissed. That is a third position in this corpus.
> Smart Money Decode X builds its whole method on the sweep and the change of
> character; the Vorwald channel calls stop-hunting a retail myth and points
> at single-digit contract prints below the lows where the hunters should be.
> Carmine's answer is that it does not matter which story moved the order, as
> long as you can see the order. He is not neutral in practice, though: in
> episode 4 the stop hunt is one of the places he says institutions buy at
> wholesale.

**If I had to start again** *(20:34)*

> "Reading this volume and reading this information through the order flow is
> the best strategy one could use. And if I had to go back to 2016 I would
> start with this. I would remove the indicators, I would remove the holy
> grail, and I would read the market as an auction. Look at it this way and
> see how volume affects price, because this will be the truest picture of the
> market, and we can represent all of this through a normal candlestick
> chart."

> **Editor —** The framing throughout is "the only day trading strategy you
> would ever need", and it is a premise, not a strategy. Nothing here tells
