## In brief

Carmine's two everyday instruments are the depth of market and the Bookmap
heatmap, and this episode is his account of what each column and colour
actually means. The DOM's value, he argues, is that completed transactions
cannot be faked: they are "the market's receipt". Resting bids and offers can
be pulled in a millisecond, so he gives them little weight on their own; what he
watches is aggression meeting size, and the delta it leaves at one price. There
is no threshold. An outlier is whatever stands out from the ladder around it.

Four ES trades from autumn 2024 illustrate the method, every one a winner,
every one planned at three to eight times its risk. The episode is also where
he makes the heatmap a core tool, which sets him squarely against the Vorwald
channel's claim that a heatmap shows where orders sat but not what was done.
Neither side brings statistics, so the corpus cannot settle it.

## The argument

Carmine's premise is the one that runs through the whole series: a candle is a
picture of volume, and the depth of market is where the volume is. The ladder
shows both sides of every transaction, who was aggressive and who was passive,
and everything else on a chart is downstream of that.

What he adds in this episode is a ranking of trust across the ladder's columns.
Completed transactions come first, because they cannot be faked: "they are the
market's receipt". The DOM aggregates them for the whole session, with no
timeframe and no bar to close. Delta at a price comes second, buy-side volume
less sell-side, and he insists there is no threshold for it; he wants an
outlier against whatever the surrounding rows are printing. Resting bids and
offers come third, and he shows why with a bid he could place and
pull in a millisecond. The one use of resting size is watching what happens
when an aggressor meets it, and that shows up on the completed side anyway.

The first journaled trade puts the ranking to work. A downtrend line on the
one-minute ES chart, and before the break the ladder shows nothing: flat
profile, no volume tail, delta unremarkable. The break prints +1633 at 5810,
buyers the aggressors. He marks the price, watches it hold through six, seven,
eight retests by his count, and enters on the hold rather than the break. The
reasoning is worth more than the trade: a large buyer at 5810 does not want
price far below his fill and will defend it, so "the market doesn't find
support there just because support is there". The journal shows a 1.75-point
stop, 10.25 points and $15,375 on 30 contracts, 5.86R. He is candid that a
volume tail formed at the highs while he was in, that it meant the auction had
not decided, and that later in the session it decided against the move.

Then the heatmap, and with it the conflict this episode carries. Bands are
resting liquidity, thicker as they warm from blue to red; bubbles are executed
aggression, green hitting the offer, red hitting the bid. He says more than
once that it is the DOM in a different picture. The Vorwald channel's heatmap
video says the opposite: institutions and its own group do not rely on
heatmaps, because a band tells you where a large order sat but not how it was
executed, in what chunks or at what speed, and the order book is the true
market. Two practitioners, one object, opposite verdicts, and no statistics on
either side. It is a narrower dispute than it looks. Carmine's own yellow line,
a bid that never traded and was pulled as price fell, is the Vorwald objection
in Carmine's mouth, and his weight is on the bubbles, not the bands. What
remains contested is whether bubbles carry pace. He credits pace to the DOM,
never to the heatmap.

His breakout framework is two steps overlaid on the heatmap: confirm strong
volume in the direction of the trade, then require the pullback to hold with no
rejection. A large buyer or seller is any price, or cluster of prices, with
outlying volume or delta, to be watched on the retest. This is episode 7's CLC
rule with the labels off, context, location, confirmation on the live tape, and
it is also the idea-and-proof entry the Vorwald channel teaches in its
volume-profile video: pullback after the move, not on it.

The second trade is the same price twelve days later, with +4000 delta at 5810
that shrinks to +3300 as sellers try and fail. Delta at a price can only fall
if sellers hit it, so a shrinking positive delta with price holding is sellers
losing. A 3.5-point stop, $21,750, 4.14R.

The last two trades are the reversal side. A September short into buyers who
bought a breakout that failed, which is the trapped-participants slide from
episode 2 and the absorption the Vorwald channel derives independently. Then
the October long the series calls a stop hunt: a trendline breakdown with no
volume tail at the high and little selling on the break, so he buys where
retail is stopping out. The Vorwald channel calls stop hunts a retail myth, on
the evidence that footprints show single-digit prints under lows. That is
Carmine's evidence too. They agree on the tape and disagree on the word.

What the episode leaves out is what the series leaves out: a win rate, a
loser, a sizing rule. Four winners at planned 3.29R to 8.33R, stops of 1.5 to
3.5 points beyond the level, targets of 9.75 to 13 points. The Vorwald channel
supplies the corpus's only paired hit rate and risk-reward, and its "maybe a
two" is a hedge, not a floor.

## In his words

**What the ladder is made of** *(01:00)*

> "Now we have to ask ourselves: what makes up a candlestick chart? As the
> candles go down, as the candles go up, this is all represented by volume
> coming into the market. Now, the tool on the right is what's called a depth
> of market, and this is the most powerful tool in the market, because it tells
> us the depth of the market. It tells us everything that we need to know in
> relation to volume, in relation to price, in relation to who is aggressive and
> who is passive."

**The market's receipt** *(02:45)*

> "Look directly under this, down here at 5749 and three quarters. We see 61 buy
> contracts, or buy lots, on the offer and 12 on the sell side, or on the bid
> side. So 61 buyers, 12 sellers. This gives us a total volume of 61 plus 12 is
> 73. So the first, most important concept of a depth of market, and why this is
> so important, is because it shows completed transactions, which cannot be
> spoofed, or which cannot be faked. They are transactions. They are the
> market's receipt. They hit the market at that specified price. Now this is all
> aggregated, meaning as the day is moving forward these numbers will increase
> as more volume builds up. There isn't a time frame on the depth of market.
> There isn't a specific range. This is aggregated by all volume at that price
> for the full session."

**Delta at a price** *(03:41)*

> "The next column is VPD. This is a volume profile delta. We've learned delta
> in episode two of this order flow series, which essentially is ask volume
> minus bid volume. Down here at 5743 and a half there is 415 total volume on
> the offer, or towards the buy side, and 704 on the bid side. 415 minus 704
> gives us a volume delta of 289."

**Why resting orders get little weight** *(04:41)*

> "What we can now see is bid volume and ask volume. So these now are just
> passive participants in the market. They do not show intention. These can be
> spoofed, these can be faked. In fact, I do not put much emphasis on any
> resting orders or any orders that get displayed on the level two or the order
> book. This is liquidity at specified prices, because what I could do right
> now is go throw a bid on the market at 5751. Maybe I throw 30 contracts, and
> right now there's a bid for 27. If I throw 30 on the market, now we see we
> have 57. I can remove my 30 in a millisecond, and I could look like there's a
> strong buyer there, but really I have no intention to buy it. So passive
> orders on the bid or the ask do not show intention. I do not put much
> emphasis on it."

> "However, it is important to look at if an aggressive participant comes into
> a very large passive participant, and we can validate this and confirm this,
> because if a transaction gets complete where there's a large passive
> participant and a large aggressive participant, and they come together and
> agree and transact, well, we'll be able to see that on the completed
> transaction side, whether it's a buy or sell. And then we'll also see the
> delta also build, depending if it's more buying or if it's more selling, and
> also the total volume will build. So you'll see it all compensate, and they
> complement each other."
