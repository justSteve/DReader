# Read: How powerful is Volume Trading REALLY?

> **The channel's answer to "is volume high today?": compare the opening minutes and the closing auction against the same windows on earlier days, then let the weekly profile and the weekly close say whether fair value is holding or shifting.**

- **Channel:** Tom Vorwald EN · **Uploaded:** 2025-12-04 · **Duration:** 13:00
- **Card:** [CARD.md](CARD.md) · **Watch:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YkclL6xgu-s

## In brief

The presenter's claim is that a volume number means nothing on its own. It
becomes information once you know whether it is high or low for this
instrument, and at what time of day it printed. His method for the first is
comparison, not a threshold: look at the first minutes after the open, then at
the same minutes yesterday and the day before, and you can see whether the money
that has to be placed each morning is growing or drying up. Do the same for the
closing auction, where large participants finish the orders they could not
complete during the day.

From there he moves to the weekly volume profile. A balanced week establishes
fair prices; the next week's close, and the volume traded at that close, tell
you whether those prices were rejected or merely tested. A close below value on
thin volume, with nothing traded beneath it, argues for a return to the old
equilibrium rather than a collapse. The video ends with the channel's only
plainly stated stop and target rule.

## The argument

He opens by refusing the premise of his own title. Volume does not lead anywhere
"in isolation"; it has to be set against two references before it says
anything. The first is the instrument: what counts as large on one product is
small on another, so you have to know your product's typical sizes. The second
is time of day. The trades that matter to him are the ones printed when large
participants are obliged to act, and those cluster at two moments, the open and
the close.

The opening read is the operational contribution. He does not ask whether the
first five minutes were busy in the abstract; he asks how they compare with the
same five minutes on the preceding days. Rising means more money has to be
placed and the day will have activity; falling means the market may be
"lethargic". He is careful to add that this tells him nothing about direction.
The comparison is the discipline that the channel's volume-exhaustion video
([HySZZSjMxF8](../HySZZSjMxF8/CARD.md)) never supplies, where "increased volume"
is judged by eye with no baseline at all. It is also the same move Carmine
Rosato makes for delta in his tools episode
([tgQC7Dpcc8A](../tgQC7Dpcc8A/CARD.md)): disclaim a fixed number, then build a
session baseline and call anything far outside it an outlier. Both men land on
relative rather than absolute thresholds. Neither writes the relation down as a
rule, and on this channel delta is on the histogram and read for direction only,
the gap already logged on the heatmap video
([Vd83oo_geMk](../Vd83oo_geMk/CARD.md)).

The closing auction is the second reference, and his explanation of why it
carries information is the clearest in the corpus. Institutions hold positions
through the day that must be adjusted; an order they could not finish is brought
to the auction, because that is where counterparties who want to deal in blocks
are found. So the size of the auction tells him whether large participants still
want in. He generalises from this to a premise the whole channel rests on: large
participants need sideways phases and time to secure fair prices, and dislike
volatility. That is the same auction picture Carmine draws in his foundation
episode ([QaNPAaEnB5E](../QaNPAaEnB5E/CARD.md)), passive size waiting for
aggression to come to it, stated here from the institution's side of the desk.

The weekly profile section is where the reasoning becomes a trade idea. A week
with strong buying at the bottom and strong selling at the top is balanced, and
its prices are accepted. The following week opens above that value, falls
through it, and closes below it, and the tell is the volume at that close: there
was little. The point of control, which he defines as the volume-weighted
centre of gravity, sits above. His deduction is that the market is more likely
to return to that equilibrium than to keep falling, and in his example it did.
The definition is the volume-weighted one, the VPOC that the channel's Market
Profile video ([K8qtT2_axPo](../K8qtT2_axPo/CARD.md)) separates from the
time-weighted POC, and the return-to-value logic is the value-area re-entry
rule of its companion ([1utSjHs_Mq8](../1utSjHs_Mq8/CARD.md)) run in the other
direction: a rejection that finds no new volume beneath it comes back. The weekly
close is also another appearance of the channel's close rule, stated most fully
in the PbD video ([jXR1afMy-3o](../jXR1afMy-3o/CARD.md)), though here it is an
observation on one chart, not a rule announced as such.

He then repeats the logic on a footprint chart, and the key word becomes
acceptance. If the market trades below a large-volume area and builds real
volume there, it is accepting lower prices and the downtrend is more likely to
continue; the same holds above. A pullback into the area where liquidity has
shifted upward is where he would look for another long. That is Carmine's
break-and-retest in the volume-episode form
([w7tvJCuZAq8](../w7tvJCuZAq8/CARD.md)): never the initial break, always the
pullback that proves the participants are still there. It is also the
idea-and-proof entry the channel teaches in its volume-profile video
([NkQeOVDTAec](../NkQeOVDTAec/CARD.md)), the level first, the confirmation
second. Where he differs from Carmine is in what confirms: Carmine wants the
live tape, this presenter is content with volume having shifted.

The close supplies the rule the card flags. Stop below the last volumes; target
where large volumes traded in the past, "with high probability". Both are
volume-defined locations, which puts him nearer Carmine's support-and-resistance
episode ([5qBo04SMUFc](../5qBo04SMUFc/CARD.md)), where a level is real only
because the market's order flow made it so, than to a drawn line. No distance,
no risk-reward and no hit rate accompany the rule, and there is no trade in the
video. The examples are static screenshots, and the ones with figures on them
are not S&P charts; those figures are cut from this read and the reasoning is
kept.

## In his words

**Why volume is never read alone** *(00:52)*

Today I want to tell you why I don't analyze trading volumes or turnovers in
isolation. I analyze them because in the end it doesn't lead you to the point you
need in order to logically deduce something meaningful from trading volumes. What
really matters for you first? You need to know: are the trading volumes for the
respective instrument high or low? At what times are these trades taking place?
And if you then refer, for example, to older time frames where you can check how
things were at the same time yesterday or the day before yesterday at the same
time and so on, then you get a better overview of whether the trading volumes
are declining, staying constant or even increasing. And then you get indications
as to whether these trading volumes can actually be useful for your goals.
Because whenever trading volumes decrease, supply and demand are no longer as
high. This means that fewer large market participants are interested in being
active in the markets at that time.

**The opening minutes, against the same minutes yesterday** *(01:57)*

Let's start by simply getting an overview of whether trading volumes remain
consistently stable at the respective times or not. Some of you might be
thinking right now, what kind of boring nonsense is this? But I want to show you
something and it's really, really important. For example, here [...] we have the
market opening and these are the trading volumes, even recorded in delta. That
means I can see whether for the corresponding volumes there were more purchases
or sales made. So what information do I have now? I have the information that in
this time frame about [...] contracts were traded in the first 5 minutes after
the market opened. Now, just imagine the day before at the market opening,
[...]. The day before that, maybe you even had [...]. What does that
immediately show me? It immediately shows me that the inflow of funds that needs
to be placed in the market right in the morning is decreasing, or conversely,
it's increasing. And whenever that's the case, I know there's liquidity that
needs to be placed in the market.

But I still don't know whether the markets will rise or fall as a result. In
short, this information tells me simply a hint as to whether I should expect
activity or whether the markets are more lethargic or slow because no one is
interested anymore.

**The closing auction** *(03:26)*

The second thing I really like to look at is what are the trading volumes during
the closing auction. [...] That's the moment [...] when the futures prices are
settled at the cash close. This means that the earlier physical trading on the
stock markets comes to an end at that point. Now, you need to know that many
large institutional investors buy or sell their positions during the closing
auction. Positions they still hold during the day and need to adjust. What does
that mean? It means that if, for example, as a large trader you have the order
for today [...] to place [...] shares on the market, and over the course of the
day you've managed to place [...] then you'll bring the remaining shares to the
market during the closing auction, because it's precisely in the closing
auctions that you often find trading partners who want to buy or sell in large
blocks.

So the information tells me how many contracts are currently being traded in the
closing auction. It's always a good indicator for me to know whether there is
still a lot of demand from large market participants to enter the markets or
not. You have to understand, large market participants need sideways phases like
here so they can position themselves in the market at fair prices. Large market
participants need time to secure fair prices in the market. They really don't
like it when the markets are extremely volatile. And that's the reason why both
the market opening, to see what's happening in the morning, and especially the
closing auction give me very important indications of how I should assess the
volume. And that's the first building block you should get to know in order to
develop an awareness for it.

**A week of fair prices, and the week that rejected them** *(05:24)*

Let's take it a step further and look at these profiles here. This is market
profiling and volume profile, each viewed over a period of one week. And now, as
I said, it's not enough to just look at the volumes here. For example, you can
see the point of control from this volume profile. What else can I see? I can
see that there were strong buys down here, but also strong sells at the top. So
I immediately recognize that the supply and the demand can be considered as
being fairly well balanced. So I know that as long as the market fundamentally
stays within this particular zone here, as we just observed, the fair and
equitable prices are initially accepted by participants, and that's good.

So what happens in the following week? And that's really, really good to take a
look at. The market opened above this, above the fair price level, and then it
dropped and even closed down here. What indication does that give me? And here
you really have to look beyond the obvious. [...] As we just looked at, down here
is the closing price for this week. And we said that closing prices are an
important indication for us to examine how the market is positioned at the end
of trading. And what you can see here for this week at this closing price level,
there was little trading volume. And the logical consequence from this, since we
also have the point of control, that is the volume weighted center of gravity,
up here, the market moved back up again the following week. Why does it do that?
Because if we go back to the major market participants, they are always looking
for locations where supply and demand are in balance.

But that by no means implies that the markets will collapse or anything like
that. It simply gives us an indication that fair prices are shifting downward.
And if I now have the following information, on the one hand a rejection at the
fair prices and on the other hand, and this is the essence, the most important,
a shift of the fair prices to lower levels, this situation gives me the
opportunity to first determine, okay, there is real selling pressure because the
trading volumes are decreasing. However, if I then notice that the market below
these fair price levels, below the value areas, does not have any significant
trading volume, and in addition that not much happened at the closing price this
Friday either, then I can deduce from this that there are probabilities, meaning
that the market, with a higher likelihood than continuing to fall, will first
return to this equilibrium level. And such information is extremely useful for
us because there is logic behind it.

**The same logic on a footprint** *(08:38)*

Let's also take a look at the whole thing in a footprint chart. Because here too
the logic is no different. [...] Up here we lost interest. Here, on the other
hand, the trading volume was highest in this price range. Below that, everything
was a bit less again. And down here the trading volume was very high again. What
does that mean when I look at it? For me this means the information I need now
is: what happens if we move above these volumes? What happens if we go below
these large volumes? So is the market interested in establishing new fair
business below that? So can we expect real volume there at lower prices? Yes,
then the market is simply doing nothing other than accepting lower prices. And
greater acceptance of lower prices means that for now the probability is high
that the downtrend will continue. It's the same to the upside when we move over.
And you can see it beautifully here. As liquidity shifts upwards, there's simply
a high probability that the market, as long as it doesn't take out these deeper
real volumes anymore, will move from here to the upside. And as you can see here,
this is a price area where I need to watch, if I get a pullback in the market,
whether it might be a good idea to take another long trade from here. And all of
this comes from the simplest logic of supply and demand.

**What it adds up to** *(10:14)*

So we understand that openings and closings, that is the determination of
opening and closing prices, happen in the cash time frames, are very, very
important and interesting, because we understand why the big market
participants like to use these times and hours to conduct their business. We
understand that by analyzing the volume profiles we can determine and identify
where fair pricing is. Then when you combine that together with the
corresponding closings, this gives us awareness. Awareness of how we can
anticipate when the market is striving to return to equilibrium or when it is
rejecting it. And last but not least, we can very nicely observe whether it
really is the case that when we get a setup at, for example, such a price level,
the market actually tells us yes, I accept the higher prices, or tells us no, I'm
not interested. And for that I need to look at what happened at the top, what
happened at the bottom, and how can I piece together a matrix from that to make
the whole thing smart.

**Where the stop goes, and where the target** *(11:24)*

Let's explain the whole thing again in a really simple way. Let's assume a market
moves like this. And here I am. So what do I need now? Now I need the
information: where were there large volumes traded? Let's just say there were
large volumes here and the market comes back down here. So what do I have to do
now? I have to check whether the volumes have really shifted upwards, and then I
can wonderfully look for a new entry for myself.

And these are the simplest approaches, where in the end you just have to look
out for smart setups so that you can clearly and easily determine: where does my
stop loss belong? Yes, below the last volumes. And where are my targets? With
high probability, there where there used to be large volumes in the past. And
that's the result when you can correctly count the volumes with the
corresponding volume data, interpret them properly, and know which volume sizes
are typical for each instrument. For example, S&P future [...]. So we need to
know when are they actually large volumes, and we need to know when volumes are
rather small.
