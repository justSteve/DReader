# Read: Fair Value Gaps — Why Price Always Comes Back (And When It Does Not) (Video 13)

> **The series' account of what aggression leaves behind: three candles that record levels price skipped rather than negotiated, an exact zone to mark instead of a vague area of interest, and a tendency to return that the presenter is careful to call a tendency and not a rule.**

- **Channel:** Smart Money Decode X · **Uploaded:** 2026-09-08 · **Duration:** 12:37
- **Card:** [CARD.md](CARD.md) · **Watch:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GzCq0tvFzhU

## In brief

A fair value gap is what aggression leaves behind. When enough market orders arrive at once to consume the resting limit orders at several price levels simultaneously, price jumps rather than walking, and the levels it skipped were never negotiated. Three candles record it: the middle one moves hard enough that the third candle's low never comes back to the first candle's high, and the space between those levels is the gap.

The presenter's claim is that price tends to return to those levels, because the market tends toward efficiency and the participants who missed the move still want in. The load-bearing word is *tends*: not every gap fills, context decides, and a gap is evidence rather than a magnet. What it buys you is precision, an exact top and bottom to watch instead of a vague area of interest, and one entry rule the series keeps arriving at — the arrival is the setup, the reaction inside the zone is the signal.

## The argument

This video is the sequel to a sentence dropped seven videos earlier. In video six the order book was the engine of price, and a fair value gap appeared as a definition and nothing more: a "void where no resting limit orders" sit, drawn as a mock book with a hole above the last bid and one resting sell order far above it ([GGe2widuvPs](../GGe2widuvPs/CARD.md)). Price crosses that hole in a single transaction because there is nothing in between to match against. What video six never said is what happens next, and this video's whole contribution is the claim that price tends to come back.

The mechanism is restated rather than assumed, which is the best part of the video. Normal movement is market orders matched against resting limit orders at each level, which is why candles overlap: something at every price slows the move and forces interaction. A large concentration of aggressive orders arriving at once consumes several levels simultaneously, price jumps to the next resting orders, and the levels in between were traded through "as a consequence of order imbalance, not as a result of orderly negotiation". That is order-flow reasoning rather than pattern recognition, and it is the reasoning Carmine Rosato opens his series with, splitting an auction into balance and imbalance ([QaNPAaEnB5E](../QaNPAaEnB5E/CARD.md)). For Carmine the imbalance is the *transition* between two fair-value ranges, aggressive buying lifting price until a new range forms, with no claim that the traversed range gets revisited. Here the traversed range is the object of the whole lesson.

The Vorwald channel prints the same event a third way. A low-volume node is "price zones where the market slips into imbalance" ([jXR1afMy-3o](../jXR1afMy-3o/CARD.md)), read off a volume profile as the thin band where almost nothing traded ([YmygDgtoxO8](../YmygDgtoxO8/CARD.md)). Three schools, three instruments of observation, one hole in the record. Neither of the other two makes the return claim; this video does, and it should be read as this channel's proposition rather than the corpus's.

To its credit it hedges properly: not every gap fills, context determines whether price returns, and many never fill at all on higher timeframes during strong trends. The hedge survives into the first of the four mistakes, where the gap is demoted from magnet to evidence. The one place it slips is a spoken sentence claiming the tendency "is measurable and observable across every market and every time frame". Nothing measured appears anywhere in the video: no hit rate, no sample, no backtest.

The four significance factors are where the video does real work: displacement size, timeframe, whether the structural context supports the gap, and whether price has already been into the zone without reacting. The third ties this video to the series, since a gap left by a displacement that also broke structure inherits the structural narrative, which is exactly video ten's third mark of a strong break of structure: a clean pullback to a logical level, an order block or a fair value gap ([YwqfGuktXrc](../YwqfGuktXrc/CARD.md)). The fourth introduces a decay rule the series has not had before, each entry that produces no reaction reducing what the zone is worth.

The confluence section cites "the order blocks you learned in video 12". Neither video 12 nor video 11 is in this corpus — the playlist has grown past the ten carded in the series synthesis — so neither is linked here. The pairing is already on the record anyway: video eight placed the order block as the candle right before a swing point and the fair value gap as the space within the move, which is precisely the "two different things, same area" this video builds its strongest-evidence case on ([d2RzSJC98o8](../d2RzSJC98o8/CARD.md)).

Two definitions close the argument. "Mitigated" means price entered the zone and produced a meaningful reaction, so full penetration is not required; a candle that closes through the whole gap without reacting means the expected buying interest was not there, which the presenter frames as new information rather than failure. Then the entry rule: entering the gap is the setup, the reaction inside it is the signal. That is where Carmine dissents most sharply, since his confirmation lives in the live tape, never in a completed candle ([7facFfjQ0UE](../7facFfjQ0UE/CARD.md)), and his standing rule is to let a break happen and buy the pullback rather than the break itself ([w7tvJCuZAq8](../w7tvJCuZAq8/CARD.md)). On the candle question this video is squarely with video four, where the close is the verdict of the negotiation ([oktlv1rOG9Q](../oktlv1rOG9Q/CARD.md)).

What is missing is what the series always leaves out. "Significantly larger" is never quantified, nor a "healthy pullback", nor how far a confirmation candle must close. There is no stop, target or risk figure, and no real instrument: every chart is an AI mock, and the one labelled with an index has its "significant" gaps shaded over whole pullbacks rather than three-candle gaps.

## In his words

**The gap you have already seen** *(00:00)*

> "Have you ever watched price move so fast in one direction that it left a visible gap on your chart? A space between candles where price never actually traded, and then later, sometimes hours, sometimes days, price came back and filled that exact space before continuing in the original direction. That is not a coincidence. That is not random market behavior. That is a fair value gap, and understanding why it forms, why price tends to return to it, and how to use it as evidence in your trading is one of the most practically useful skills you can develop."

> **Editor —** "Tends to return" and "as evidence" are in the opening sentence, which is more discipline than the title shows. Hold the channel to the hook rather than the headline.

**Three candles** *(00:40)*

> "A fair value gap is a three candle pattern. It forms when a middle candle moves so aggressively in one direction that it creates a space between the wick of the candle before it and the wick of the candle after it. In a bullish fair value gap, the low of the candle after the aggressive move is higher than the high of the candle before the aggressive move. That space between them is the fair value gap. Price moved through that area so quickly that it left an imbalance, a zone where buyers and sellers did not have adequate opportunity to interact at those prices. In a bearish fair value gap, the high of the candle after the aggressive move is lower than the low of the candle before it. Same concept, opposite direction."

> **Editor —** Note that the definition is wick to wick, not body to body. Some ICT-derived teaching uses bodies; this one does not, and the slides agree with the narration on it.

**Normal movement, and then not** *(01:41)*

> "When price moves through a level with normal participation, market orders are being matched against resting limit orders at each price level. This process creates the typical candle by candle movement you see on most charts. Each candle overlaps slightly with the previous one because there are resting orders at each price level that slow the movement and create interaction. But when a very large concentration of aggressive orders hits the market simultaneously, when a significant participant places a large market order or a cluster of closely timed orders, the resting orders at multiple price levels get consumed all at once. Price jumps from where it was to where the next available resting orders exist. The levels in between, the ones that were skipped, are the fair value gap. No normal buyer seller interaction happened there. The price moved through those levels as a consequence of order imbalance, not as a result of orderly negotiation."

> **Editor —** This is the video's best paragraph and the reason the concept is worth anything: the gap is not a shape, it is a record of a book being cleared faster than it could refill. It is video six's mechanic ([GGe2widuvPs](../GGe2widuvPs/CARD.md)) told from the chart's side, and it is Carmine's imbalance ([QaNPAaEnB5E](../QaNPAaEnB5E/CARD.md)) and the Vorwald channel's low-volume node ([jXR1afMy-3o](../jXR1afMy-3o/CARD.md)) described in a third vocabulary. The slides illustrating it are decoration: the drawn order book stacks its buy size on the wrong side of price, and the "cannon" firing through a ladder is a cartoon.

**Why price tends to come back** *(02:45)*

> "This is why fair value gaps tend to be revisited. The market has a general tendency toward efficiency. Levels where price never had adequate two-sided interaction tend to attract price back at some point as participants who missed the initial move seek to interact at those price levels. This does not mean every fair value gap gets filled. Context determines whether price returns to a gap or continues without filling it. But the tendency exists and it is measurable and observable across every market and every time frame."

> **Editor —** The first four sentences are the argument and they are fairly stated. The last one is not: nothing in the video measures anything. It is the one place where a hedge is quietly upgraded into a claim of evidence, and the card records that no statistic appears anywhere. Worth noting too that neither of the other two schools that describe this event makes any return claim at all — they describe price moving *fast* through the zone and stop there.

**Marking it** *(03:25)*

> "Look for three consecutive candles where the middle candle is significantly larger than the ones around it and moves strongly upward. The bullish fair value gap is the zone between the high of the first candle and the low of the third candle. If those two levels do not overlap, if there is a clear space between them, you have a bullish fair value gap. Mark that zone from the high of candle 1 to the low of candle 3. That entire zone is the area of imbalance."

> **Editor —** "Significantly larger" is doing a lot of unpaid work. Video eight ([d2RzSJC98o8](../d2RzSJC98o8/CARD.md)) is the one place in the series where a "significant" is turned into countable candles, and it does that job for swing points only. Nothing here tells you how much larger the middle candle has to be, which means the first step of the procedure is a judgement call dressed as a measurement.

**When price returns** *(04:00)*

> "After the initial move that created the gap, price often retraces, it comes back towards the gap. If the bullish structural context is still intact, if you are in a bullish trend, if the gap is aligned with the higher time frame direction, then when price enters the fair value gap zone from above, it may find the buying interest that missed the initial move. The gap acts as a support zone. Price enters the gap, encounters the buying pressure, and resumes the upward move. The fair value gap gives you a specific defined zone to watch, not a vague area of interest. A specific high low range that you can mark precisely and watch price interact with. That precision is one of the most valuable aspects of this concept."

> **Editor —** The honest case for the whole idea is in the last three sentences, and it has nothing to do with institutions or smart money. A gap gives you two exact numbers, a top and a bottom, where "an area of interest" gives you a smudge. Everything else in the video is a probability claim; this bit is just better bookkeeping. The slide that illustrates it works a zone with an exact top and bottom on a mock instrument, and those figures are left in the [card](CARD.md).

**The mirror** *(04:51)*

> "In a bearish fair value gap, the zone is between the low of the first candle and the high of the third candle. Same three candle logic, opposite direction. [...] When price rallies back into that zone from below in a bearish structural context, it may encounter the selling pressure that missed the initial downward move. The gap acts as a resistance zone."

**Not every gap is equal** *(05:24)*

> "This is where most traders who learn fair value gaps make the same mistake. They mark every gap on every time frame and treat them as equally significant. They are not. [...] The first factor is the size of the displacement that created it. A fair value gap created by a massive [...] move on the daily chart carries far more significance than a gap created by a [...] move on the 5-minute chart. The larger the displacement, the more aggressive the order flow that created the gap and the more meaningful the imbalance it represents. The second factor is the time frame. A fair value gap on the daily or 4-hour chart is a significant structural imbalance that many participants will be watching. A fair value gap on the one minute chart is a minor imbalance that may fill within seconds or never be revisited meaningfully."

> **Editor —** The reasoning behind factor one is sound and is the same reasoning as the mechanism: a bigger jump means more levels cleared and more orders that never got filled. The worked comparison is on a foreign-exchange example and is cut here; it is in the [card](CARD.md).

**Structural context, and freshness** *(06:29)*

> "The third factor is the structural context. A bullish fair value gap that forms within a clear bullish trend created by a displacement that also produced a break of structure is supported by the structural narrative. A bullish fair value gap that forms against the direction of the higher time frame trend is fighting the structural context and is less likely to act as meaningful support when price returns to it. The fourth factor is whether the gap has been tested before. A fresh fair value gap, one that price has not yet returned to, carries more potential significance than one that has already been partially or fully entered and failed to produce a meaningful reaction. Each time price enters a gap and does not produce a clear reaction, the significance of that gap diminishes."

> **Editor —** Factor three is the join to the rest of the series: video ten ([YwqfGuktXrc](../YwqfGuktXrc/CARD.md)) already counts a clean pullback to an order block or a fair value gap as one of the three marks of a strong break of structure, so the two videos are describing one event from opposite ends. Factor four is new and it is the most practical rule in the video, because it kills the habit of treating a zone as fresh forever. The slides put a number on the decay by drawing a bar that halves and quarters, but no threshold is stated in speech.

**Order block and fair value gap** *(07:19)*

> "The most powerful application of fair value gaps is in combination with the order blocks you learned in video 12. In many cases, the fair value gap and the order block sit in the same general area. The order block is the last candle before the displacement. The fair value gap is the space left by the displacement itself. Together, they define a zone of interest that has multiple layers of evidence supporting it. When price retraces into an area where both an order block and a fair value gap overlap, you have two separate pieces of evidence pointing to the same level: an order block suggesting large participant activity at that location and a fair value gap suggesting an imbalance that has not yet been resolved. Two pieces of evidence are more compelling than one."

> **Editor —** Video 12 is not in this corpus and neither is video 11; the playlist ran on past the ten that were read, so the order-block lesson is cited here without a card behind it. The definition survives on its own, and it matches the one video eight ([d2RzSJC98o8](../d2RzSJC98o8/CARD.md)) gave in passing: the order block is the candle right before, the gap is the space within the move. "Two pieces of evidence are more compelling than one" is the sort of claim that sounds like arithmetic and is not — the two are produced by the same displacement, so they are one observation counted twice, not two independent ones.

**What filling actually means** *(08:20)*

> "Filling a fair value gap does not necessarily mean price trades through the entire gap from one boundary to the other. A fair value gap can be considered mitigated when price enters the zone and shows a meaningful reaction. If price enters the lower half of a bullish fair value gap and produces a strong rejection, a long lower wick, a bullish engulfing, a strong close back above the entry level, that reaction provides evidence that the imbalance is being addressed and buyers are present at those levels. Full penetration to the opposite boundary of the gap is not required for the gap to have served its purpose as a support zone. Similarly, if price enters the gap and closes through the opposite boundary without a meaningful reaction, the gap has been fully mitigated and the structural context needs to be reassessed. A gap that price closes through completely is evidence that the buying interest expected at that level was not present in sufficient quantity to hold price. This is new information."

> **Editor —** Two useful things at once. First, "mitigated" is defined by reaction rather than by geometry, which is the right way round and rarer than it should be in this material. Second, a failure is treated as data instead of as bad luck: the zone did not hold, therefore the buyers you inferred were not there. That is a falsifiable reading of your own analysis, and the series is at its best when it does this.

**Not a magnet** *(09:33)*

> "The first mistake is treating fair value gaps as guaranteed fill zones. A fair value gap is evidence of an imbalance. It is not a magnet that must pull price back. Many gaps never fill, particularly on higher time frames during strong trending moves. The structural context determines the probability of a fill, not the existence of the gap alone."

> **Editor —** This is the answer to the title. "Why price always comes back" is the thumbnail; "many gaps never fill" is the lesson. Take the second half of the title, "and when it does not", as the real subject.

**Structural fog** *(09:57)*

> "The second mistake is marking every small gap on every time frame. This creates the same structural fog problem we discussed with swing points. Focus on gaps that formed from significant displacement moves on meaningful time frames. A gap that took 3 seconds to form on a 1 minute chart is not the same evidence as a gap that formed from a daily displacement move."

> **Editor —** "Structural fog" is video eight's phrase, borrowed intact ([d2RzSJC98o8](../d2RzSJC98o8/CARD.md)) — there it was too many swing points, here too many gaps, and both times the cure is the same: mark fewer things. The slide meant to illustrate the cure is the weakest in the video, an AI-drawn index chart whose "significant" gaps are shaded over entire pullback ranges rather than three-candle gaps.

**Setup and signal** *(10:23)*

> "The third mistake is entering a trade simply because price enters a fair value gap. Price entering the gap is the setup. The reaction within or at the boundary of the gap is the signal. Wait for the candle to show you what price is doing at the gap before you act. A candle that enters the gap and shows immediate rejection is very different from a candle that enters the gap, sits in the middle, and continues lower through the gap boundary."

> **Editor —** The most transferable sentence in the video, and the corpus's clearest fault line runs right through it. This channel puts confirmation in a completed candle, consistent with video four's rule that the close is the verdict ([oktlv1rOG9Q](../oktlv1rOG9Q/CARD.md)). Carmine reads confirmation on the live tape and says explicitly that a candlestick chart does not tell him when to enter ([7facFfjQ0UE](../7facFfjQ0UE/CARD.md)), and his other standing rule is to never take the initial break, only the pullback ([w7tvJCuZAq8](../w7tvJCuZAq8/CARD.md)). Both sides agree on the principle of waiting; they disagree about what you are allowed to wait for, and the cost of the disagreement is measured in how far price has already moved by the time you act.

**Static analysis of dynamic markets** *(10:49)*

> "The fourth mistake is ignoring the gap once it has been partially mitigated. A gap that price has already entered and left without filling completely is a weaker piece of evidence than a fresh gap. Update your analysis as price interacts with your marked zones. Static analysis of dynamic markets is one of the most common causes of trading mistakes."

**The framework** *(11:11)*

> "When price displaces significantly and leaves a fair value gap, mark the zone precisely. Note the structural context. Is the gap aligned with the trend? Note whether an order block exists in the same area. Wait for price to return to the zone. Observe the reaction within the zone. Not when price approaches, but when it actually interacts with the boundaries. If the reaction confirms the expected behavior based on the structural context, that is your evidence to act. If the reaction contradicts the expected behavior, if price enters a bullish gap and immediately closes below the bottom boundary, that is new information that changes the structural picture."

> **Editor —** "Not when price approaches, but when it actually interacts with the boundaries" is the whole discipline in one clause, and it is the thing most people get wrong: the zone being touched feels like the event, and it is not the event.

**Not magic** *(11:55)*

> "Fair value gaps are not magic. They are observable evidence of price imbalance. The market has tendencies. One of those tendencies is to return to areas of imbalance. Your job is to identify those areas precisely, evaluate the context that determines whether the tendency is likely to play out, and act on the confirmation of that tendency rather than the anticipation of it."

> **Editor —** A better closing statement than the video's own title. Precision, context, confirmation — the same three-step shape Carmine calls context, location, confirmation ([7facFfjQ0UE](../7facFfjQ0UE/CARD.md)), reached from an entirely different direction and with no tape in sight.
