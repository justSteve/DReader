# Read: The ONLY Volume-Profile-Video you will EVER need

> **The one video in either order-flow spine of this corpus that states a win rate, a risk-reward target, and the sample size needed to trust them — in the same breath.**

- **Channel:** Tom Vorwald EN / WorldClassEdge · **Uploaded:** 2026-04-29 · **Duration:** 9:33
- **Card:** [CARD.md](CARD.md) · **Watch:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NkQeOVDTAec

## In brief

Nine and a half minutes on volume profile, built around a two-part rule the presenter calls **Idea and Proof**: the profile gives you a price level where a reaction is *possible* (the Idea), and a local setup — some formation in price, volume, or footprint — gives you evidence the reaction is happening now (the Proof). The trigger he actually trades is a pullback into that setup range after a breakout, not the breakout itself. What separates this video from a routine walkthrough is its last two minutes: he states a sample-size floor (200–300 trades), a planning hit rate (50%), and a risk-reward target — the only place in this corpus where those three numbers appear together. The arithmetic behind them is sound. The championship branding wrapped around the lesson is not verified, and one of the stated numbers is softer in his own words than the card's summary of it suggests.

## The argument

The presenter starts from a premise he never proves but states plainly: well over 95% of the volume printed in the market is placed by large institutional participants — pension funds, hedge funds, players with an obligation to route size, not a view to express. Retail's edge, on his account, isn't prediction; it's watching where that obligated flow went. It's a rhetorical foundation more than a measured one — no source, no data — and it does real work for the argument that follows, so it's worth flagging as a claim rather than treating it as established.

From there the teaching is genuinely good. Volume profile is nothing more than summed volume displayed at each price. He marks the high-volume clusters, shows price reacting there on the chart, and states the limit of what that buys you with unusual honesty: a level tells you a reaction is *possible*, not that it will happen. That admitted limit is what forces the rest of the method — since a level alone isn't enough, he needs a second, independent signal that the reaction is actually underway. That's the Proof: a setup, defined as "a formation of price or volume or a footprint or whatever that gives me an advantage in keeping my risk smaller." It's a clean definition because it's about risk, not direction — a setup earns its place by shrinking the stop, not by calling the move.

This is where the corpus connects. Idea-then-Proof is structurally the same move Carmine Rosato's series calls **CLC** — Context, Location, Confirmation — with Idea mapping to Location and Proof to Confirmation. Neither source cites the other; both arrived at "you need a level, then you need independent evidence the level is live" from opposite starting points. Where they part is what counts as evidence. Carmine insists on the live tape — footprint and DOM, read in the moment, never a candle close. This presenter accepts a candlestick pattern (his example: an inside bar sitting on light volume) as sufficient proof. It's the more accessible version of the same theory, and the trade-off is that his evidence bar sits lower than Carmine's.

The entry itself agrees with Carmine on a more specific point: it's a **pullback after the breakout**, never the initial break. That's Carmine's break-and-retest, arrived at independently. It's also worth noting where it disagrees with the *same* channel's own material — the one-minute scalping video on this channel teaches entry on the break itself, with no published rule anywhere for when to use which. That's an internal inconsistency, not a corpus dispute, but it means a viewer who's only seen one of the two videos gets half the channel's own doctrine.

The last two minutes are the video's real contribution. He asks whether the viewer actually knows their own numbers for a given setup — and most don't — then supplies his own: at least 200 to 300 trades before a statistic means anything, a hit rate assumed at 50% for planning purposes, and (in his words) an average risk-reward "maybe a two." The Carmine series runs eight episodes and three and a half hours without ever stating a win rate anywhere; this single passage closes that gap for the corpus. The numbers also cohere: at a 50% hit rate, break-even sits at a risk-reward of exactly 1.00, so an average near two leaves roughly half a unit of expectancy per trade — real margin, not a coin flip dressed up. Set beside this same channel's other claim of an 80–90% win rate that never once states its risk-reward, this video is the more complete — and more falsifiable — of the two.

One caution on precision: the card summarizes the risk-reward figure as "greater than 2," but what he actually says is softer — "maybe a two." That's a hedge, not a floor, and it's worth keeping the hedge rather than reading a firmer claim into it than he made.

## In his words

**What the profile actually is** *(01:48)*

> "The first thing I can see here is the volume at the corresponding price levels that occurred over here. That means nothing more than summed up volumes. I'm having them displayed here for the respective prices. So, how does that help me? At first, not at all."

> **Editor —** Most volume-profile explainers open by asserting the tool's predictive power. He opens by denying it, then earns the claim back over the next two minutes.

**Why levels react, and the limit of that** *(02:30)*

> "Where did we have high volume? For example, here… the market has reactions there. And why do reactions take place there? A lot of liquidity was simply traded there by major players. First, we note that where high volume occurs, we often see a reaction when a market returns there. What can we plan for ourselves now? We can only ever expect that something will happen there. That doesn't mean exactly what we plan will happen, only that something could happen."

> **Editor —** That last sentence is the honest core of the video, and it's the sentence that justifies everything after it: if a level is only a maybe, something else has to confirm the reaction is live.

**Idea and proof** *(03:11)*

> "For us, it clearly means we need an idea. We've now created that idea in a very simplified way. And in the second step, we need proof that at this price level, where we have an idea, a corresponding reaction from the players also occurs. So, always an idea and proof that the idea is working out."

> **Editor —** This is Carmine Rosato's CLC rule under different labels — idea is his location, proof is his confirmation. Both channels arrived here independently; neither one names the other. The difference is what each accepts as proof, and that difference matters more than the shared structure.

**Against the one-size-fits-all setup** *(03:39)*

> "And now the question is always, which setup do we want to use there? And now there are many who always say, we always use the same setup. And that is, of course, nonsense."

**What a setup actually is** *(04:41)*

> "A setup is nothing more than a formation of price or volume or a footprint or whatever that gives me an advantage in keeping my risk smaller."

> **Editor —** Note what this definition is *not* about: direction. A setup earns its place by shrinking the stop, not by predicting the move. That reframing is the most exportable idea in the video.

**The setup illustrated, and the entry** *(04:54–05:41)*

> "Let's zoom in here. Let's say this here is alpha. That's the price area I'm using as a setup… Let's assume we had a candlestick pattern — an inside bar, which means nothing more than the market lingering there, leading to the market making a decision, so a short pause. Let's further assume this setup is backed by low volumes, just as we need it… If the market moves away from that particular level after this setup is finished, and I get a pullback, then I can look to enter right here at this point in the market."

> **Editor —** The entry is the pullback *after* the move, not the break itself — Carmine's break-and-retest. It also contradicts the entry-on-the-break taught in this same presenter's one-minute scalping video, with no stated rule anywhere for choosing between the two. Across the channel's catalogue, though, the pullback entry is the more common teaching.

**Do you know your figures** *(06:42–07:19)*

> "Do I really know my key figures, for example, for this setup in combination with this high-volume-to-high-volume strategy? Most people don't know them, and that's why you need key figures — and you have to make at least two to three hundred trades to have key figures. And if you should now, for example, reach a hit rate of 50% with that — and what does hit rate mean? That means entry until the market has arrived down here. Then you really have clarity, because then you know: ah, my average risk-reward ratio is maybe a two. And so you have a big advantage in the market."

> **Editor —** The one passage in this corpus where a sample size, a hit rate, and a risk-reward target appear together. At 50%, break-even is exactly 1.00 R, so an average near two leaves roughly +0.5 R of expectancy per trade — genuine margin, arithmetically. Keep the hedge in his own words, though: he says "maybe a two," not "greater than two." The firmer number belongs to a summary of this passage, not to the sentence itself.

**Extending it to other timeframes** *(07:40)*

> "You can also do the same with intraday profiles, for example. Or you use other corresponding markets… Here we had a profile, for example, a weekly profile. Did it give me a setup here? I don't see that in hindsight at all. But I can plan if a market runs up here that I expect a reaction up here."

**Closing the loop** *(09:03–09:23)*

> "I can clearly track all kinds of setups for myself, but you have to do that. And if you don't do that, trading is always like flying blind… Of course, you also have to accept your stops. Otherwise, without discipline, everything is for nothing."
