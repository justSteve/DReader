## In brief

Volume profile, taught from first principles and then pushed somewhere most
explainers never go. The presenter starts where everyone starts — summed volume
at each price, high nodes and low nodes — and immediately concedes that on its
own this tells you nothing. What it gives you is a place where a reaction is
*possible*. That distinction carries the whole video.

From there he builds a two-part discipline he calls **idea and proof**: the
profile supplies the idea, a level where something might happen; a setup at that
level supplies the proof that it is happening now. Neither half is sufficient.
Then he does the thing almost no trading video does — he asks whether you know
your own numbers, and answers it with figures: at least 200 to 300 trades before
your statistics mean anything, a hit rate around 50%, an average risk-reward
around two.

It is nine and a half minutes and it is the most quantitatively honest video in
this corpus.

## The argument

He opens with the premise the whole method rests on: more than 95% of the volume
you can read is placed by large participants — pension funds, hedge funds — and
they are not expressing a view so much as discharging an obligation. They *must*
route orders. Retail's advantage is not prediction but observation: you can see
where that flow went. He is explicit that this is not a licence for invention,
and takes a swipe at "hocus-pocus setups" and "wild indicators" along the way.
That anti-indicator stance he shares with Carmine Rosato, who makes the same
argument from the other direction — that indicators are downstream of order flow
— and with Trading Notes, whose whole pitch is a chart with nothing on it.

Then the honest part, and the reason this video is worth reading rather than
summarising. Having drawn his high-volume levels and shown price reacting at
them, he refuses to overclaim:

> *"We can only ever expect that something will happen there. That doesn't mean
> exactly what we plan will happen, only that something could happen."*

Set that beside the rest of the corpus. Another video on this same channel
promises "Perfect Entries (Every Time)". A third claims an 80–90% win rate
without ever stating the risk-reward that would make it meaningful. AlgoTrade
Pro reports a Sharpe ratio of 4.19 next to a 75% drawdown. Here is the same
presenter saying a level tells you only that *something could happen* — and
building the method around that limit instead of hiding it.

The method follows from the limit. If a level only tells you something might
happen, you need a second, independent confirmation that it is happening — hence
**idea and proof**. The idea is the level, planned in advance, with a route from
one high-volume node to the next. The proof is a setup at that level, and his
definition of a setup is the best sentence in the video: a formation that gives
him an advantage in keeping his risk smaller. Not a formation that predicts
direction — one that shrinks the stop.

That two-step is Carmine's CLC rule under different names: idea maps to
*location*, proof to *confirmation*. The two men are describing one procedure and
neither cites the other. Where they part company is the evidence each accepts as
proof. Carmine wants the live tape — footprint and DOM, read in "the now".
This presenter will take a candlestick pattern, an inside bar, a volume shelf.
That is the accessible branch of the same theory, and its cost is that his proof
is weaker than Carmine's.

The last two minutes are the video's real contribution. He asks whether you know
your key figures for this setup, in this market, and observes that most people
don't. Then the numbers: at least **200 to 300 trades** before your statistics
mean anything; a **50% hit rate**; and at that hit rate an average risk-reward of
about **two**. The Carmine series runs eight episodes across three and a half
hours and never states a win rate at all — our own synthesis records the absence
explicitly. This one slide closes that gap. And the numbers cohere: at a 50% hit
rate you break even at a risk-reward of exactly 1.00, so an average around two
leaves roughly half a unit of risk in expectancy per trade. Real margin, not a
knife edge.

He closes on discipline — that you have to accept your stops, or none of it
matters — and on the alternative to keeping figures at all, which he calls flying
blind.

## In his words

**On what the profile actually is** *(01:48)*

> "The first thing I can see here is the volume at the corresponding price levels
> that occurred over here. That means nothing more than summed up volumes. I'm
> having them displayed here for the respective prices. So, how does that help
> me? At first, not at all."

> **Editor —** Worth pausing on "at first, not at all". Most volume-profile
> explainers open by asserting the profile's predictive power. He opens by
> denying it and then earns it back.

**Why levels react** *(02:30)*

> "Where did we have high volume? For example, here… And as you can already see,
> the market has reactions there. And why do reactions take place there? It's
> quite simple. A lot of liquidity was simply traded there by major players…
> First, we note that where high volume occurs, we often see a reaction when a
> market returns there."

**The limit of what a level tells you** *(02:55)*

> "We can plan, for example, that there are other price levels where we can
> simply localize. Should the market reach that point, we can initially expect a
> reaction. That's all we can do. We can only ever expect that something will
> happen there. That doesn't mean exactly what we plan will happen, only that
> something could happen."

> **Editor —** This is the sentence the rest of the corpus needs and mostly
> lacks. It is also the load-bearing justification for the two-step method that
> follows: if a level is only a maybe, you need something else to tell you when.

**Idea and proof** *(03:11)*

> "For us, it clearly means we need an idea. We've now created that idea in a
> very simplified way. And in the second step, we need proof that at this price
> level, where we have an idea, a corresponding reaction from the players also
> occurs. So, always an idea and proof that the idea is working out."

> **Editor —** Carmine Rosato calls this CLC — context, location, confirmation.
> Idea is his location; proof is his confirmation. Same procedure, no shared
> vocabulary, no cross-citation. The difference is what counts as proof: Carmine
> demands live tape, this presenter accepts a chart formation.

**Against the one-size setup** *(03:50)*

> "And now the question is always, which setup do we want to use there? And now
> there are many who always say, we always use the same setup. And that is, of
> course, nonsense."

**What a setup is** *(04:41)*

> "A setup is nothing more than a formation of price or volume or a footprint or
> whatever that gives me an advantage in keeping my risk smaller."

> **Editor —** The definition is about *risk*, not direction. A setup earns its
> place by shrinking the stop, not by predicting the move. That reframing is the
> most portable idea in the video and it applies to every strategy in this
> corpus.

**The entry, and the risk that comes with it** *(05:38)*

> "If the market moves away from that particular level after this setup is
> finished, and I get a pullback, then I can look to enter right here at this
> point in the market… We have a very clearly definable risk here. Many people
> don't do it like that at all, but let's do it."

> **Editor —** Note the entry is on the **pullback after** the move, not on the
> break itself. That is Carmine's break-and-retest, and it contradicts the
> entry-on-the-break taught in this same presenter's one-minute scalping video.
> Across his catalogue the pullback entry is the dominant teaching.

**Do you know your figures** *(06:58)*

> "Do I really know my key figures, for example, for this setup in combination
> with this high-volume-to-high-volume strategy? Most people don't know them and
> that's why you need key figures, and you have to make at least two to three
> hundred trades to have key figures. And if you should now reach a hit rate of
> 50% with that — and what does hit rate mean? That means entry until the market
> has arrived down here — then you really have clarity, because then you know:
> ah, my average risk-reward ratio is maybe a two. And so you have a big
> advantage in the market."

> **Editor —** The one passage in this corpus where a presenter states a sample
> size, a hit rate and a risk-reward together. At 50% you break even at exactly
> 1.00, so an average near two leaves about +0.5 units of risk per trade in
> expectancy. Note he says "maybe a two" here where the on-screen slide reads
> *greater than* two — the spoken version is the more modest of the pair.

**The alternative** *(08:40)*

> "I can clearly track all kinds of setups for myself, but you have to do that.
> And if you don't do that, trading is always like flying blind."

> "Of course, you also have to accept your stops. Otherwise, without discipline,
> everything is for nothing."
