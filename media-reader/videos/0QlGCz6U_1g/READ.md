# Read: STOP Thinking Like A Retail Trader, Start Trading Like An Institution

> **Carmine's premise episode: retail is the bait, patient money buys it at wholesale inside the stop hunt and sells it back at market price on the breakout. The corpus's plainest statement of a stop-hunting thesis that two other sources dispute.**

- **Channel:** Carmine Rosato · Trading Orderflow Series, ep. 4 · **Uploaded:** 2024-10-29 · **Duration:** 16:25
- **Card:** [CARD.md](CARD.md) · **Watch:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0QlGCz6U_1g

## In brief

Carmine opens by disowning his own title. He does not like "retail versus
institution"; he prefers Warren Buffett's line about money moving from the
impatient to the patient, and the episode is that sentence unpacked. Impatient
traders buy the breakout, buy the trendline hold, buy the strong green candle,
and are stopped on the pullback just before the market goes where they said it
would. Those stops are the liquidity. Patient money buys from the stopped-out
sellers at what he calls wholesale and sells to the chasers at market price.

The one piece of tape is a Bookmap replay of the 2 October 2024 ES session: a
cascade of red sell bubbles under a trendline, then a column of large green buys
straight off the low. Ep. 3 journals that session as a trade. This is the
clearest statement in the corpus of the stop-hunt thesis that the Vorwald
channel calls a myth and Trading Notes half-accepts. No numbers are given: no
win rate, no threshold, no sizing.

## The argument

He starts with vocabulary. "Retail versus smart money" is the
language of the genre; he says twice he is not a fan of it and substitutes
impatient versus patient. That moves the claim from *who* the other side is to
*how* they behave, and behaviour is something a tape can show. Goldman Sachs is
his picture of patience, 236 profitable days against 15 losing ones in 2012,
and the posture he draws from it is the point: our job is not to compete with
these people but to trade with them.

How does retail trade? Two chart examples from the same ES morning. Buy the
resistance break; it pulls back; you are stopped; it then breaks for real. Long
the trendline because "the trend is holding"; it dips through; you are stopped;
two minutes later it rallies. His diagnosis is the episode's first real
teaching: "a line on your charts will not change your life." The line causes
nothing. What it does is tell you where a crowd will act.

That is the hinge. Rule one is to always know who is on the other side of your
trade; his practice is to ask, before entering, how the people already
positioned feel at this price. Answer that and you know where the pain is, and
pain is where the stops are. Then the pricing frame: the breakout above
resistance is buying at market price; the stop run below the trendline is
buying at wholesale. "Instead of getting long when the trend line is holding, I
rather get long when there's a stop hunt."

He then shows it in order flow: Bookmap, ES, 2 October 2024. Red sell bubbles run down from about 5747 to 5741 as the trendline
gives way, the longs being stopped; a column of big green buys then climbs from
about 5742 to 5754.5, and his entry line goes in at about 5742.
[Ep. 3](../tgQC7Dpcc8A/CARD.md) journals this session as a trade: long from
5742.5, stop 5741, target 5755, eleven points. This episode is the reasoning;
ep. 3 is the ticket. What joins them is [ep. 7](../7facFfjQ0UE/CARD.md)'s CLC
rule, context then location then confirmation, with confirmation read off the
live tape and never from a candle close. Ep. 4 supplies the location half and
leaves confirmation to the Bookmap.

The second half is persuasion rather than method. Would you buy the stock at
the green box? No: price is falling, lower lows, buying after a crash is bad,
bad earnings. The box is demand. The car analogy that follows is
[ep. 1](../QaNPAaEnB5E/CARD.md)'s New Car Auction slide brought back as feeling
rather than diagram.

Then the bait. When price rallies, retail buys where institutions are selling;
when it drops, retail sells where institutions are buying. Retail and big money
are the auction, and "big money has a need to stay ahead of retail traders for
their orders to be filled." Here sits the corpus's largest independent
convergence. The Vorwald channel's
[volume-profile video](../NkQeOVDTAec/CARD.md) argues that more than 95% of
readable volume is placed by large participants, pension funds and hedge funds,
who must route orders regardless of price, and that retail's
edge is planning around them rather than predicting. Neither cites the other.

The conclusion is not shared. Carmine's fishermen see the support break, know
retail will get short, and "absorb all this liquidity": they are hunting. The
[Vorwald podcast episode](../RwQBdF9TSvc/CARD.md) calls stop-hunting "a retail
trading myth" and offers evidence: footprints show single-digit contract trades
printing below chart lows, so there is no institutional size down there doing
any hunting, and the ICT sweep-for-liquidity story is "hyped up and
inaccurate"; a [third Vorwald video](../aursfDVYzUk/CARD.md) credits the fast
moves to institutional futures hedging. [Trading Notes](../IUWvHVout94/CARD.md)
splits the difference, putting "NOBODY IS HUNTING YOUR STOPS" on screen and
then building its strategy on stops being harvested mechanically. Three
positions on one question: the hunt is real and it is where I buy (Carmine);
the hunt is not there (Vorwald); nobody intends it, but it happens and you can
trade it (Trading Notes). Carmine's Bookmap frame is the only tape in the
exchange, and large buying *after* the red cascade is consistent with all three
readings. The disagreement is about who
was on the red bubbles, and a bubble does not carry a name.

He closes with the guard rail the naive version needs: not short every rally
and buy every dip, not mechanically do the opposite of what you would have
done, but ask where the crowd is about to be in pain and let the tape confirm
it. What the episode never gives is a number: no win rate, no delta threshold,
no position size, the gap the whole
[eight-episode series](../../playlists/orderflow-series-PLWQioWs8oOiFKQnwIIYbw8N7ks7d-UAPQ.md)
carries and that only the Vorwald volume-profile video partly fills. Next episode: which support and resistance levels deserve trust.

## In his words

**Impatient versus patient** *(01:08)*

> "Now, I'll be honest, I'm not the biggest fan of saying retail versus
> institution or retail versus smart money. Let's use the famous Warren Buffett
> quote about the stock market is a device for transferring money from the
> impatient to the patient. So, let's call impatient traders versus patient
> traders. Now, it's obvious 95% of retail traders lose and a good portion of
> why a lot of traders struggle is because of their impatience."

> **Editor —** The 95% here, and the "90% of day traders fail" that opens the
> video, are unsourced and appear in no card. Treat them as rhetoric. The
> reframe is the useful part: it turns an identity claim into a behaviour claim,
> and behaviour shows on a tape.

**Retail is the liquidity** *(02:21)*

> "Retail traders essentially provide liquidity for other smart money
> institutional traders, big pockets, to buy from us when we're selling or if
> retail selling, institution patient traders are buying from us. So, let's look
> at Goldman Sachs performance 1 year. They had 236 profitable days in one
> single year and only 15 days of losses. … They made 18.1 billion dollars from
> sales and trading. 41 of those days, they made over 100 million dollars. …
> Our job is to not compete with them. We don't want to be in competition. We
> want to trade with them, follow their footsteps."

> **Editor —** The slide is a Bloomberg excerpt for 2012. Nobody has checked
> it, and the histogram on the same slide has losing-day bars that sum to 16
> against the headline 15. It is doing motivational work, not analytical work.

**How retail trades** *(03:42)*

> "The first thing that comes to my mind is buying when they should be selling
> and selling when they should be buying. For example, a very simple strategy
> that I've seen majority of traders, especially in the beginning stages, fall
> into is a simple little resistance break or a pre-market high breakout or a
> simple lower day breakdown or pre-market low. As soon as this low breaks, I'm
> going to get in the market. This, while can be effective, is not as effective
> in the long run, not putting an emphasis on the data and not putting an
> emphasis on specific market conditions."

**Stopped out, then it goes** *(04:54)*

> "How many times have you stopped out of a position for a loss and then the
> market eventually moves in your favor? You buy the market, it's breaking out
> to the upside, you get in long as it's breaking out, it then pulls back on
> you. And on this pullback right here, you stop out of this trade for a loss.
> You're frustrated, you're like, 'What did I do wrong?' And then a few minutes
> later, the market continues moving up."

**A line will not change your life** *(05:45)*

> "Let me tell you one thing for a fact, a line on your charts will not change
> your life. What will change your life is consecutive and consistent
> repeatable trades that you could approach the market every single day. So,
> let's just say you see in the market hold to this trend line, you're like,
> okay, the trend's holding, let me take the market long because this trend is
> holding. Then the market pulls back on you and you stop out of this trade for
> a loss. … And then, 2 minutes later, the market rallies and your original
> analysis or your original plan would have made money if you did not stop
> out."

> **Editor —** The Vorwald podcast goes further than this and says support and
> resistance "do not truly exist as fixed price points". Carmine's claim is
> narrower: the line is not the cause, it is a place where a crowd will act.
> His [ep. 5](../5qBo04SMUFc/CARD.md) spends half an hour deciding which lines
> deserve trust, so he is not with Vorwald on the strong version.

**The market makes buying look like selling** *(06:37)*

> "I expect the market to do something rather than the market telling me what
> to do. How many times have you longed the market just because you saw a
> really strong green candle to the upside? You know, we do this every single
> time and look at every single time, the move eventually fails. The market
> will make buying look like selling and selling look like buying. In this
> example, they make it look like buying for the market to then rally and then
> dump and stop out the original traders that are longing these moves up."

**Who is on the other side** *(07:16)*

> "Number one, do not think conventionally. Always know who is on the other
> side of your trade. Who is buying from you? Who is selling from you? What I
> tell myself before I enter any trade is if I'm looking to become bullish, I
> try to question if I am long here, how are other participants feeling if they
> are also long here? Or if the market's selling off and I know shorts or
> sellers are factored in at the high and I'm looking to short this move. I ask
> myself, how are the sellers that got short or sold before me feeling at
> prices now? … And this is an edge in itself because when you do this, you
> understand where there's going to be pain, you understand where other traders
> are going to be targeting, stopping out at, and this provides a solid edge
> and an advantage."

> **Editor —** This is the seed of ep. 7's CLC rule. "Where there's going to be
> pain" is location; whether the pain is actually being taken is confirmation,
> and ep. 7 insists that be read on the live tape, never a candle close. Here
> he only names the question.

**Market price versus wholesale** *(08:03)*

> "A big one is market versus wholesale prices. Don't you want to sell at
> market price and buy at wholesale prices? Buying this breakout above a
> resistance level is essentially buying a market price. You want to be buying
> a wholesale price at a discount. That way you can sell at market price for a
> profit. So, if the markets ever break in a resistance or a support level, I
> am first looking for where there's a lot of buying because I know where
> there's a lot of buying in the market, I should be selling. Or especially
> when there's a lot of obvious trades that I know a lot of retail traders are
> watching because it's so simple to spot on a chart. Or the classic stop run.
> Instead of getting long when the trend line is holding, I rather get long
> when there's a stop hunt cuz now I am buying at wholesale prices, and then I
> could sell at retail prices."

> **Editor —** "Sell at retail prices" is a slip; everywhere else the pair is
> wholesale and market. Kept as spoken.

> **Editor —** This is the sentence the rest of the corpus argues with. The
> [Vorwald podcast](../RwQBdF9TSvc/CARD.md) calls stop-hunting a retail
> trading myth, on the evidence that footprints show single-digit contract
> trades printing below chart lows, so there is no institutional size down
> there doing the hunting; it calls the ICT sweep-for-liquidity story hyped up
> and inaccurate. [Trading Notes](../IUWvHVout94/CARD.md) puts "NOBODY IS
> HUNTING YOUR STOPS" on screen and then teaches: identify the pool, wait for
> the sweep, enter on confirmation. Carmine says the hunt is real and it is his
> entry. Three sources, one question, three answers, and none cites the others.

**Read the volume behind the candlesticks** *(08:52)*

> "And the only way we're going to be able to spot this information is by
> reading the volume behind the candlesticks. This cannot fully be developed or
> understood or learned from a candlestick alone. … putting yourself in the
> shoes of those traders who are facilitating these transactions, this is where
> the edge comes from by reading the volume behind the candlesticks that I've
> been mentioning in all the previous videos in this order flow series."

> **Editor —** What is on screen while he says this: Bookmap, ES, 2 October
> 2024, stamped 10:03:39. Red sell bubbles cascade from about 5747 down to
> about 5741 as the white trendline breaks, the biggest at 5743 to 5744; a
> column of large green buy bubbles then runs from about 5742 up to 5754.5. His
> green entry line sits at about 5742 and the annotation reads "Classic stop
> hunt. Can prevent these stop hunts by reading the volume behind the candle
> sticks…". [Ep. 3](../tgQC7Dpcc8A/CARD.md) journals this same session as
> trade 4: long 5742.5, stop 5741, target 5755, eleven points. The frame shows
> buying after the stops printed, which every side of the stop-hunt argument
> accepts; it does not show who sold into them.

**Would you buy here** *(10:03)*

> "If you see the market selling off, would you buy the market down here as
> it's aggressively moving lower? And the answer to this that majority of
> people would say is no. Price is coming down. Price is making lower lows.
> Price is making lower highs. So, we're obviously in a downtrend. Buying after
> a crash is bad. The stock market's crashing. Covid just hit. Doesn't make
> sense. … A lot of traders don't like buying when there's a lot of fear or
> there's a lot of bad sentiment. But, in my opinion, that's the best edge and
> that's where the best opportunities are because that's when there's going to
> be a lot of emotional decisions from the impatient traders."

> **Editor —** The slide asks "Would you buy the stock at this green box?" over
> a chart that runs 2908 to 2940 with Wednesday and Thursday dividers; the box
> is labelled Demand. The instrument is never named.

**The car** *(10:52)*

> "Let's say you're in the market for a car. In the first episode of this order
> flow series, we related it to like a market's auction. … If you see the price
> of this car selling off … and every day it's getting cheaper and cheaper and
> cheaper. Would you ask yourself, if you're in the market for that car, would
> you now buy it? … A lot of you will now say yes because look, car's on sale.
> I could buy it at a wholesale price. It's cheaper than it was a couple of
> days ago, and I would like to buy it before prices maybe go back up. So, when
> you put a good like a car or a house or a watch here, it makes total sense. …
> We would want to buy any good at wholesale price. Wholesale is going to be at
> discount. Wholesale is going to be at a price that not the market is paying.
> We don't want to pay what everybody else is paying. For us to profit, we have
> to pay or buy when nobody else or minimal people have an opportunity to buy
> at such a discounted price."

> **Editor —** The callback is to [ep. 1](../QaNPAaEnB5E/CARD.md)'s New Car
> Auction slide, where fair value moves from $35,000 to $40,000 to $36,500 as
> buy and sell imbalances resolve. That is the balance-and-imbalance model;
> this is its emotional twin.

**Imbalance to balance, wholesale to market** *(12:33)*

> "This is how price moves and this is how price changes. It goes from
> imbalance to balance and wholesale to market. A lot of retail traders buy
> market price and sell wholesale price. We have to flip the equation and buy
> wholesale and sell market price."

**Who is buying from retail** *(12:49)*

> "When the market moves up, retail traders see the move up and they
> automatically get so bullish or they automatically think the thing is going
> to go to the moon and they're going to make a lot of money just because they
> see a green candle or the market's coming up. They are buying. If they are
> buying, you have to ask yourself, who is selling? As we've learned from the
> previous videos in the series, for every transaction, there's a buyer and
> there's a seller. So, if retail's buying, who's buying from retail? Majority
> of the time, people could say market makers, but let's just say patient
> traders or smart money is selling when they know majority of retail traders
> are buying. And then price drops aggressively and a lot of retail traders see
> this aggressive sell-off to the downside, and they start selling, not
> realizing that if they're selling, somebody else has to be on the other side
> of their trade. Who is that? And this could be the patient traders."

> **Editor —** He declines to name the counterparty; "market makers" is waved
> away for "patient traders". The Vorwald channel's
> [volume-profile video](../NkQeOVDTAec/CARD.md) names them: pension funds and
> hedge funds placing more than 95% of readable volume, who must route orders
> regardless of price. Same premise, from an entirely separate lineage.

**Bait** *(13:38)*

> "We want to be trading with patient traders because when we're patient, we
> let the bait come to us. When we're impatient, we are the bait, and we're
> swimming around getting caught up and providing liquidity for other traders
> who are the fishermen outside of the water looking for us to bite the bait.
> So, we don't want to be liquidity, we want to trade with liquidity. And we
> want to be the fishermen, not the bait swimming in the water buying when we
> should be selling and selling when we should be buying."

**Retail and big money are the auction** *(14:04)*

> "Traders do not understand how dynamic the auction process of the market is.
> Retail and big money are the auction. Big money has a need to stay ahead of
> retail traders for their orders to be filled. If they have to buy, they want
> retail to sell. If they have to sell, they want retail to buy. They do not
> want competition. We are their bait. Outsmart the fishermen. Think of big
> market players, call them patient traders, as the fishermen. They see the
> support level breaks, they know, 'Hey, as soon as the level breaks, I know
> majority of retail traders are going to get short.' They absorb all this
> liquidity. If majority of retail traders are selling, who is buying? The big
> market patient traders."

> **Editor —** "A need to stay ahead of retail for their orders to be filled"
> is the Vorwald channel's routing argument in Carmine's words: size has to get
> filled, and retail is what it fills against. Where they part is the next
> sentence. Carmine's fishermen absorb the sells below the level; the Vorwald
> podcast says the prints below the level are single-digit lots, too small for
> anyone to be absorbing. The diagram on screen makes his side vivid, thirteen
> or so fish each labelled STOPLOSS under a bank with a fishing rod, but a
> diagram is not a footprint.

**Not the automatic opposite** *(14:50)*

> "I want to make this very clear. This does not mean every single move up
> you're looking to short. And every single move down you're looking to buy.
> This does not mean that. This just means put yourself in the shoes of other
> retail traders. When you know other traders are stopping out, when you know
> other traders are buying a breakout, break down, think twice, and think how
> those traders could be put in pain because where there is pain, there will be
> gain, and this is how we come into the market and profit off of it. … Don't
> just think that you're going to do the automatic opposite. Hey, this is a
> trade that I normally buy and since I normally buy this and I'm wrong, I'm
> going to automatically sell it. This is not the point of this video. This is
> for you to switch your mindset and to start thinking like a patient trader
> and to stop trading like an impatient trader."

> **Editor —** The best teaching in the episode and the reason it is not a
> contrarian system. Contrarian is still prediction. What he is describing is
> waiting for the crowd to be trapped and then reading whether it is; the
> reading is ep. 7's confirmation step, and this episode does not supply it.
